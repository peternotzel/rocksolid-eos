# The Visionary and the Integrator

At the top of the Accountability Chart in the Entrepreneurial Operating
System™ (EOS™), Wickman draws a distinction that almost no traditional org
chart captures: the work of running a growing business is naturally divided
between two very different kinds of seats, the Visionary and the Integrator.
Roughly half of companies have both seats filled by two different people;
the other half have only an Integrator. Either configuration can work. What
does not work is forcing one person to be both, or pretending that a single
"CEO" box on the chart resolves the tension. The two roles call for almost
opposite strengths, and most people who are great at one are mediocre at the
other.

The relationship between these two seats is the load-bearing partnership at
the top of the company, and the Accountability Chart visually marks it
differently from every other line on the chart for a reason: it is not a
reporting relationship.

## The Integrator role

The Integrator is the seat that holds the front-line functions together.
Where each function head is responsible for a single discipline — sales,
operations, finance — the Integrator is responsible for the seams between
those disciplines. Friction naturally appears between functions as each one
pushes its own agenda; the Integrator's job is to take that friction and
turn it into forward momentum rather than letting it become political drag.

Concretely, the Integrator:

- Owns the day-to-day execution of the business
- Holds P&L accountability
- Owns the overall business plan
- Removes obstacles so function heads can do their jobs
- Resolves cross-functional disputes that the function heads cannot resolve
  among themselves

Wickman deliberately uses the word "Integrator" instead of any standard title
because titles vary wildly across companies — CEO, President, COO, Managing
Director, General Manager — while the functional role is the same. The
important distinction is that Integrator is a *role definition*, not a label
on a business card. Whoever holds it is doing the integrating work
regardless of what the company calls them.

The Integrator role is required. There is always exactly one. There is no
configuration where a healthy EOS company has zero Integrators or two
Integrators sharing the seat.

## The Visionary role

The Visionary is the seat that appears above the Integrator when a company
has someone whose strengths genuinely fit that profile — almost always a
founder, owner, or co-owner. The Visionary profile is in many ways a mirror
image of the Integrator profile:

- Highly creative and idea-generative — Wickman describes Visionaries as
  producing many new ideas a week, most of which are not great but some of
  which drive the company forward
- Strong on big, ambiguous, ugly problems that nobody else wants to touch
- Weak on tactical follow-through and operational discipline
- Emotionally attuned to the company's culture
- Typically the holder of the company's most important external
  relationships — major clients, key partners, the brand voice in public

The Visionary seat is **optional**. About half of companies have one. Not
having a Visionary is not a defect. The Integrator alone can sit at the top
of the chart when there is no distinct visionary profile to fill, and many
healthy companies operate that way.

## Why the partnership works

The two seats work together precisely because their strengths are
complementary and almost non-overlapping. The Visionary supplies the lust:
new ideas, strategic vision, energy, the willingness to chase the big
opportunity. The Integrator supplies the discipline: focus, prioritization,
follow-through, the ability to say "not this quarter" to the Visionary's
seventh great idea of the week. Visionary energy without Integrator
discipline produces a company that pivots constantly and ships nothing.
Integrator discipline without Visionary energy produces a company that runs
beautifully but slowly atrophies because nobody is pulling it toward
something bigger.

Together, lust plus discipline equals traction.

## The dotted-line convention

On the Accountability Chart, the line connecting the Visionary box to the
Integrator box is rendered differently from the lines connecting the
Integrator to the function heads. The Visionary–Integrator connector is
typically dotted or visually lighter. The connectors below it — Integrator
to Sales, to Operations, to Finance — are solid.

This is a deliberate visual choice. The Integrator does not "report to" the
Visionary the way a function head reports to the Integrator. The two seats
are peers in a partnership, with the Integrator running the business and the
Visionary sitting alongside as the strategic and creative complement. Any
template that flattens this distinction into a single solid line is
misrepresenting the relationship and will cause confusion the first time
the Integrator and Visionary disagree about something operational.

## Diagnosing your top of chart

The two most common failure patterns at the top of the chart:

### Visionary-only with no Integrator

A founder who is unmistakably a Visionary but has no Integrator to partner
with. Symptoms: the company has many great ideas in the air at any given
moment but ships few of them. Decisions get made and then quietly reversed.
Function heads do not feel like they have a clear escalation point for
cross-functional conflict. The team is exhausted by direction changes.

The fix is rarely "the Visionary becomes more disciplined." Visionaries are
not built that way. The fix is to find or hire an Integrator and to
genuinely hand over the keys to day-to-day execution. This is one of the
hardest transitions a founder makes because it feels like losing control. In
practice, the Visionary gains leverage rather than losing control: their
ideas finally get executed.

### Integrator promoted out of fit

The opposite pattern: a high-performing Integrator gets "promoted" to
Visionary because the company assumes that's the next step up. The promoted
person now sits in a seat where the daily work is brainstorming, big
relationships, and culture, none of which are their strengths. They become
quietly unhappy and the company loses its best operator.

The fix is to recognize that Integrator and Visionary are not steps on a
ladder. They are different jobs. Promoting an Integrator into a Visionary
seat is the same kind of mistake as promoting a great salesperson into a
sales-management seat where their strengths no longer apply.

## Criteria / Rules

- Exactly one Integrator seat on the chart, always. Never zero, never two.
- Visionary seat is optional. Approximately 50% of companies have one.
- The Visionary–Integrator connector must be visually distinct from the
  reporting connectors below the Integrator (typically dotted or lighter).
- The Visionary box should sit above the Integrator box, not beside it.
- Both boxes carry exactly five major roles.
- The Integrator's five roles always include some form of P&L
  accountability, day-to-day execution ownership, and cross-function
  integration.

## Common pitfalls

- Combining Visionary and Integrator into one "CEO" box because the founder
  insists they can do both.
- Drawing the Visionary–Integrator line as a solid reporting line — the
  team will treat it like a hierarchy and the partnership breaks.
- Promoting an Integrator into a Visionary seat as a "reward" — loses the
  best operator and gains a mediocre Visionary.
- Hiring an Integrator and then second-guessing every operational decision
  they make — defeats the purpose of the seat.
- Refusing to add a Visionary seat when the founder clearly fits that
  profile but is also being asked to run day-to-day, leading to founder
  burnout.

## When to apply

- Phase 1, when first drawing the Accountability Chart
- When a founder is exhausted and the company has stalled — diagnose whether
  the Integrator seat is missing
- When an Integrator is being considered for a "promotion" — challenge
  whether the new seat actually fits their strengths
- During quarterly reviews when cross-functional decisions keep getting
  bottlenecked at the top
