# Implementation Order of the Seven Tools

A common mistake when teams first encounter the Entrepreneurial Operating
System™ (EOS™) is to assume that vision work comes first. After all, the
Vision/Traction Organizer™ is the document the system seems to revolve
around, so it feels natural to start there. Wickman is explicit in Chapter 9
that this instinct is wrong, and he prescribes a deliberate sequence for
rolling out the seven main tools. The order is: Accountability Chart →
Rocks™ → Meeting Pulse™ (Weekly Level 10™ + Quarterly + Annual) →
Scorecard™ → V/TO™ → Three-Step Process Documenter™ → Everyone Has a
Number. The reasoning behind this sequence is practical, not theoretical, and
it shapes the structure of every implementation phase in this skill.

## Why structure comes first

The Accountability Chart sits at position one because most of the persistent
friction inside a growing business turns out to be ownership ambiguity, and
ownership ambiguity quietly weakens every other tool you try to install on
top of it. If you set Rocks before you know who owns what, the Rocks drift.
If you build a Scorecard before seat owners exist, the numbers have no real
home. If you run a leadership meeting before the leadership team itself is
defined, the meeting reinforces the wrong group. Structure comes first
because it goes to the root of the problem the rest of the tools are trying
to solve.

## Why traction comes before vision

Once seats are defined, Wickman's next move is to install the cadence and
priorities that produce visible execution: Rocks, then the weekly Meeting
Pulse, then the Scorecard. Only after the team is genuinely operating with
weekly numbers, weekly meetings, and quarterly priorities does he have them
build the V/TO.

The reason is not that vision is unimportant — it is foundational — it is
that a vision session run with a team that already lives inside a disciplined
execution rhythm produces grounded, honest, debated planning, whereas a
vision session run with an undisciplined team tends to produce aspirational
fluff that nobody is on the hook for. Once people are being measured and are
owning real numbers, planning conversations get sharper because everyone
already knows what the business can actually execute. Stretch goals stop
being theoretical. Headcount targets stop being wishes. Revenue projections
get debated against the same Scorecard the team has been staring at every
Monday for the past quarter.

Put more bluntly: a team without traction will write a vision they cannot
deliver. A team with traction will write a vision they can.

## "Pre-vision" Rocks in Phase 1

The obvious objection to this sequencing: how can a team set Rocks in Phase 1
when the Rocks are supposed to advance the 1-Year Plan, and the 1-Year Plan
lives inside a V/TO that won't exist until Phase 2?

Wickman's answer is practical. Most leadership teams already have a rough
intuition for where the business is heading — they are rarely starting from
a blank page. That directional sense is enough to set a first pass at Rocks.
The discipline of running the Rock cycle itself exposes gaps in the team's
shared understanding of priorities, and those gaps make the eventual V/TO
work far more grounded. Phase 1 Rocks are sometimes wrong; that is fine.
Their job is to teach the team how to run the cycle, not to perfectly
implement a vision that doesn't exist yet.

## The Rock Alignment Check after V/TO

Because Phase 1 Rocks are set against intuition rather than against a written
1-Year Plan, the moment the V/TO is finished in Phase 2 the team should run a
Rock Alignment Check: walk through every current-quarter Rock and ask whether
it still clearly advances something on the freshly written 1-Year Plan.
Rocks that no longer fit are candidates for replacement at the next quarterly
session. This is not a failure of Phase 1 — it is the expected handoff
between phases.

## Why Process Documenter and Everyone Has a Number come last

The final two tools are deliberately deferred. Process documentation is the
single most time-consuming activity of the seven tools (typically six to
twelve months of focused work), and it has to map cleanly onto seats on the
Accountability Chart and onto the Proven Process element of the V/TO. Both
of those have to exist first. Without operating discipline (weekly meetings,
Scorecard numbers in use), process documents get filed away and ignored;
discipline is what enforces them.

Everyone Has a Number is the very last tool because it depends on every
other tool already being in place. Pushing weekly numbers out to every person
in the company before the leadership team is fluent in measurement, before
seats are clear, and before processes have been documented produces
spreadsheet theater rather than real accountability.

## Criteria / Rules

The implementation order in summary form, with the phase each tool belongs
to in this skill:

1. **Accountability Chart** (Phase 1) — structure first, names second
2. **Rocks** (Phase 1) — pre-vision quarterly priorities
3. **Meeting Pulse** (Phase 1) — Weekly L10, Quarterly, Annual
4. **Scorecard** (Phase 1) — leading-indicator weekly numbers
5. **V/TO** (Phase 2) — full vision document, eight questions
6. **Three-Step Process Documenter** (Phase 3) — core processes at 20/80
7. **Everyone Has a Number** (Phase 3+) — measurement extends company-wide

The phase boundaries matter: a team should not advance to a later phase until
the earlier phase's tools are stable and in active weekly use, not just
authored.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with the V/TO because it feels like the "real" document — produces
  vision-as-wish-list and burns the team's appetite for the work.
- Skipping the Accountability Chart and going straight to Rocks — the Rocks
  end up owned by ambiguous people and drift within two weeks.
- Building a Scorecard before the L10 exists to consume it — the spreadsheet
  becomes nobody's job and decays.
- Documenting processes before the V/TO exists — Proven Process doesn't have
  a home and the documentation effort lacks a strategic frame.
- Pushing Everyone Has a Number out company-wide in month two — produces
  resentment and surface compliance.
- Treating phase boundaries as soft and overlapping all seven tools at once.

- Onboarding: load this block during the first session to set expectations

- Whenever a team or stakeholder asks "why aren't we starting with the V/TO?"
