# The Accountability Chart

The Accountability Chart is the first tool implemented in the Entrepreneurial
Operating System™ (EOS™), and it is the structural spine that every other
tool hangs off of. At a glance it looks similar to a conventional
organizational chart — boxes connected by lines arranged in a hierarchy — but
the question it answers is fundamentally different. A traditional org chart
answers "who reports to whom" by arranging existing names into a pyramid. An
Accountability Chart answers a different question: "what are the essential
functions this business needs to run, and who owns each function." The
ordering of those two words — function before person — is the entire point.
A team that builds the chart correctly defines all the seats first and only
afterward decides which humans go into which boxes. A team that builds it
incorrectly starts from the people they already have and ends up sculpting
seats to fit personalities.

## Function before name — the structural discipline

Wickman is emphatic that the Accountability Chart is built in a strict
sequence: draw the whole structure first, complete with all seats, all five
major roles per seat, and all the lines between seats, before any name is
placed into any box. Names only get added once the structure is finalized.

The reason is that the moment you start with names, you start rationalizing
the structure around the people you already have. A weak seat gets preserved
because someone you like sits in it. Two seats get merged because one person
currently does both. A function gets skipped because nobody obvious could run
it. All of these compromises look reasonable in the moment and all of them
lock in the exact problems the chart is supposed to fix. Separating the two
steps forces the team to first decide what the business actually needs and
only then confront who goes where.

## The vertical layers

The chart has a clear vertical structure:

- A **Visionary** seat sits at the top when the company has one — roughly
  half of companies do. It is drawn with a dashed outline to signal that it
  is optional and partnership-shaped rather than command-and-control.
- An **Integrator** seat sits directly below the Visionary. This seat is
  required and there is always exactly one. Two names in the Integrator box
  is a structural failure, not a compromise.
- Below the Integrator sit the **front-line major functions**, typically
  three to ten of them. The minimum baseline is three: a Sales/Marketing
  function, an Operations function, and a Finance/Administration function.
  Larger or more complex businesses split these out further — Sales and
  Marketing may separate, Operations may split into delivery and customer
  service, Finance may split out HR and IT.
- Any front-line function can expand downward into sub-functions with their
  own seats as the company grows.

Wickman's observed sweet spot for front-line functions is between three and
seven; he has rarely seen more than seven work well in his client base.
"Less is more" is the guiding principle.

## Visual conventions

Two visual conventions are load-bearing and worth preserving in any digital
template:

- The line connecting the Visionary to the Integrator is rendered differently
  from the lines connecting the Integrator to the function heads — typically
  dotted or lighter. This signals that the Visionary–Integrator relationship
  is a partnership, not a reporting line. The Integrator does not "report to"
  the Visionary the way a function head reports to the Integrator.
- Each box on the chart contains the seat name plus a bulleted list of the
  seat's five major roles. Boxes that show only a name with no roles are
  visually incomplete.

## The 5-roles-per-seat rule

Every seat on the chart — Visionary, Integrator, every front-line function,
and every sub-function seat below — is defined by exactly five major roles.
Not three, not seven, not a long task list: five major accountabilities that
together describe what owning this seat means. This is a firm rule in
Wickman's method, not a soft guideline.

The five roles should sit at the level of "major accountability," not the
level of "individual task." For a seat that owns a process, the five roles
might include things like ownership of the core process itself, leadership
of the team executing that process, responsibility for a defined set of
measurable outcomes, ownership of a key external relationship, and cultural
or values stewardship within the function. The exact wording depends on the
business — the rule is the cardinality and the altitude of the roles, not a
fixed template list.

## LMA — the responsibility of leadership

Anyone on the chart with direct reports inherits LMA™ — Lead, Manage, and
Accountability. "Lead" is forward-looking: clarity of expectations, direction,
team-level vision. "Manage" is present-and-backward-looking: cadence,
unblocking, day-to-day support. "Accountability" is the willingness to hold
people to commitments and have hard conversations when commitments are
missed. LMA is non-delegable. A manager who is unwilling to enforce
accountability is failing the "A" and that failure cascades down their chain
of the chart.

## A versioned, living artifact

The Accountability Chart is not a one-time deliverable. The book shows the
same fictional company's chart at multiple growth stages, and the structure
evolves substantially over time as new functions split out, sub-tiers
appear, and the leadership team grows. A healthy implementation revisits and
adjusts the chart roughly every ninety days as the business changes. Any
template should support a "version date" or "as-of" header so snapshots can
be tracked.

## Integration with the rest of EOS

The Accountability Chart is the spine that the other tools hang off of:

- The **leadership team** is defined as the Visionary (if any), the
  Integrator, and the people sitting in the front-line function seats. This
  is the group that meets in the weekly Level 10™ Meeting.
- **Rock owners** must be seats on the chart. Every quarterly priority has a
  named owner and that owner is a seat holder.
- **Scorecard™ numbers** are owned by seats. Every weekly metric traces
  back to a specific box on the chart.
- **Core process ownership** maps processes onto seats. The seat that owns a
  process is the one who also owns documenting and improving it.
- If the chart is wrong or out of date, every one of these downstream tools
  inherits the problem.

## Criteria / Rules

**Hard rules (must pass):**

- Exactly one Integrator seat.
- Minimum three front-line major functions: Sales/Marketing, Operations,
  Finance/Administration (or clear equivalents under different names).
- Every seat has exactly five major roles listed. Not four, not six.
- Structure was completed before any names were placed.
- Every person placed into a seat has Y on all three of GWC™ for that seat.

**Soft rules (context-dependent):**

- Presence or absence of a Visionary seat is optional. Roughly half of
  companies have one; not having one is not a defect.
- Number of front-line functions between three and seven is the observed
  sweet spot; more than ten is a warning sign.
- Chart revision cadence of roughly every ninety days is healthy but not a
  hard deadline.

## Common pitfalls

- Starting with names instead of seats — locks in the existing
  organizational compromises.
- Two people in the Integrator box because the team can't decide.
- Long task lists in seat boxes instead of five major roles.
- A "CEO" box at the top of the chart that conflates the Visionary and
  Integrator roles into one (forces a single person to do two very different
  jobs).
- Skipping the Finance/Administration function because the founder still
  does it personally.
- Treating the chart as a one-time artifact and never updating it as the
  business grows.
- Missing the visual distinction between the Visionary–Integrator connector
  and the Integrator–function-head connectors.

## When to apply

- Phase 1, very first leadership session — before any other tool is built
- Whenever the leadership team grows by more than one person
- Whenever a function head leaves and the team is tempted to redistribute
  the seat informally
- Quarterly review: walk the chart and confirm it still describes how the
  business actually runs
- Annual planning: rebuild the chart from scratch if the business has grown
  by more than 50% in the year
