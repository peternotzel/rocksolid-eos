# The Six Key Components

The Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™) treats every growing company as
a single machine made of six distinct but interlocking parts. The Six Key
Components™ framework is the highest-level diagnostic frame in the system: it
gives a leadership team a shared vocabulary for talking about where the
business is healthy and where it is leaking energy. The framework is rendered
visually as a six-wedge wheel with the business itself at the hub, and that
wheel reappears at the start of every component chapter in the book with the
relevant wedge highlighted. The point of the wheel is not decoration — it is
a constant reminder that the components are not a checklist to work through
once and put away. They are six interdependent surfaces that the team has to
keep strong simultaneously, because weakness in any one of them quietly
undermines the other five.

## The six components

### Vision

Getting every person in the company on the same page about where the business
is going and how it intends to get there. The primary tool of the Vision
component is the Vision/Traction Organizer™ (V/TO™), a deliberately
two-page document that compresses eight foundational questions about the
company's identity and direction into a single shared artifact. Without
Vision, leaders pull the business in incompatible directions and employees
fill the silence with their own assumptions.

### People

Putting the right people in the right seats. The People component carries
three primary tools: the Accountability Chart (which defines the seats the
business actually needs, function-first), the People Analyzer™ (which
evaluates each person against Core Values and seat fit on a single grid), and
the GWC™ filter (Get it / Want it / Capacity to do it — the three binary
tests for whether someone is in the right seat). Without strong People work,
the rest of the system collapses because there is no one to execute it.

### Data

Running the business on a small set of activity-based weekly numbers rather
than on opinion or trailing financials. The primary tool is the Scorecard™,
a 13-week rolling grid of 5-to-15 leading-indicator measurements with one
named owner per metric. The supporting principle is "Everyone Has a Number" —
the Scorecard discipline eventually extends downward so that every person in
the company owns at least one weekly measurement.

### Issues

Surfacing problems honestly and resolving them at the root rather than
firefighting symptoms. The primary tool is a continuously maintained Issues
List, which the team works through using the Issues Solving Track™ (IDS™ —
Identify, Discuss, Solve). The discipline of IDS is what turns a leadership
meeting from a venting session into a problem-solving forum.

### Process

Identifying the small set of core processes (typically 5 to 15) on which the
business actually runs, and documenting each at the 20/80 level — the
twenty percent of detail that drives eighty percent of the results. The
primary tool is the Three-Step Process Documenter™ (Identify, Document,
Compile and Distribute). The point is not to write a giant binder; it is to
make sure the structural steps of how work gets done are explicit, named
consistently, and followed by everyone.

### Traction

Bringing discipline and accountability to execution. The primary tools are
quarterly Rocks™ (3 to 7 priorities per quarter, each with a single owner
and a binary done/not-done outcome) and the Meeting Pulse™ — the cadence of
weekly Level 10™ meetings, quarterly off-site sessions, and an annual
two-day off-site that together form the heartbeat of the organization.

## Why all six matter together

The components are not independent. Each one structurally depends on the
others. A V/TO without People work in place produces an aspirational
document nobody owns. A Scorecard without an Accountability Chart produces
numbers with no real owner. Rocks without a Meeting Pulse drift by week four.
Process documents without Traction discipline get filed and forgotten.
Issues without Vision can be solved cleanly while still moving the business
in the wrong direction. The framework's central claim is that you cannot
compensate for a weak component by doubling down on a strong one — the system
runs at the speed of its weakest part.

## Criteria / Rules

A leadership team running EOS measures the health of each component on the
Organizational Checkup™, scoring twenty statements (roughly three or four
per component) on a five-point scale.

- Target state: 80% or higher overall on the Organizational Checkup.
- Below 80% in any single component is a signal that that component needs
  focused work in the next quarter.
- The Checkup is run at least twice per year (typically mid-year and at the
  annual planning session) so the team can see progress over time.
- A component score moving backwards quarter-over-quarter is itself an issue
  that goes onto the long-term Issues List.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating the framework as a checklist to "complete" rather than as an
  ongoing health surface to maintain.
- Investing heavily in one or two components (usually Vision and Traction)
  while leaving People, Data, Issues, or Process underbuilt.
- Skipping the Organizational Checkup because the team feels the score is
  obvious — the act of scoring forces honest disagreement to surface.
- Using the wheel diagram only at kickoff and never returning to it during
  reviews.
- Confusing component scores with personal performance reviews — they
  measure the system, not individuals.

## When to apply

- Onboarding: introduce the wheel during the first leadership session so the
  team has a shared frame for the work to come
- Reviews: re-score at every mid-year and annual checkup
- Triage: when a leadership team is stuck and cannot name what is wrong, the
  six-component frame surfaces the weakest area first
- Communication: use the wheel as the standing visual whenever explaining EOS
  scope to a new hire, board member, or investor
