# Marketing Strategy

Marketing Strategy is the answer to question 4 on the V/TO™ (Vision/Traction
Organizer™), the fourth tool installed by the Entrepreneurial Operating
System™ (EOS™). It is the last element of the left column on the Vision
page, and it closes the gap between the stable identity elements above it
(Core Values, Core Focus™, 10-Year Target™) and the concrete forward
picture on the right. Where Core Focus defines the sweet spot the company
operates inside, Marketing Strategy defines how the company goes to market
inside that sweet spot — which customers it pursues, why they choose it,
how it delivers for them, and what promise it makes to reduce their risk.
The section is deliberately broken into four sub-parts, each of which has
to be answered concretely enough that a salesperson could walk out of the
V/TO session and immediately act on the answers.

## The four sub-parts

Marketing Strategy has exactly four elements, and all four must be present
and substantive on the V/TO. Omitting any one of them collapses the
usefulness of the rest.

### 1. Target Market — "The List"

The first sub-part is a concrete description of the ideal customer,
sometimes called "The List" because it has to be filterable enough to
actually build a real list of named prospects. The test is operational:
could a salesperson take this description, open a prospecting database,
apply the filters, and come back with a real set of named companies or
named people to contact? If yes, the target market is sharp enough. If no,
it is not yet a target market.

The common failure is naming the target market at too high an altitude.
"Small businesses," "enterprises," "anyone who needs our product," "growth
companies" — none of these survive the filter test. A usable target market
typically combines demographic, geographic, and psychographic axes:

- A specific industry or sub-industry
- A specific company size range (revenue, headcount, or both)
- A specific role or title inside the company (the actual buyer)
- A specific region if the business is geography-bound
- Sometimes a specific stage, situation, or behavior (just raised, just
  hired a new leader, growing faster than X, using a named competitor)

The more axes are specified, the smaller and more targetable the list
becomes. A narrow list is not a limitation — it is the whole point. A
salesperson with a list of fifty exactly-right prospects closes more
business than a salesperson with a list of five thousand
vaguely-plausible ones.

### 2. Three Uniques™ — why exactly three

The second sub-part is exactly three differentiators. Not two, not four,
not five. The number is fixed and load-bearing. Three is enough to form a
defensible combination that competitors cannot honestly claim all of at
once, and few enough that the sales team and the market can actually
remember them.

The test for a valid Unique is that a competitor might be able to
honestly claim one of the three, maybe even two, but cannot honestly claim
all three together. If any competitor can truthfully claim all three, the
set is not differentiated. If none of the three would be challenged by a
competitor, the set is probably generic. The right answer lives between
those two failure modes.

Generic Uniques are the most common failure — phrases like "great
service," "experienced team," "quality work," or "we care about our
customers" are things every competitor claims. A real Unique is specific
enough to be evidenced and narrow enough that some competitors genuinely
cannot match it. The Three Uniques become the backbone of every sales
conversation, every piece of marketing collateral, and every competitive
positioning argument. Teams that try to stretch the list to four or five
dilute the memorability. Teams that compress to two lose the defensibility
of the combination.

### 3. Proven Process™ — named and visually illustrated

The third sub-part is the repeatable, named process the company uses for
every customer from first contact through ongoing follow-up. This process
has three required properties:

- **It has a real name.** Not "our process." A specific name the
  salesperson says out loud in meetings and the marketing team prints on
  the website.
- **It is visually illustrated.** A simple diagram — typically three to
  seven major steps with a handful of bullet points under each — that a
  prospect can look at and understand in under a minute.
- **It is used for every customer.** Not a highlight reel for new
  prospects and a different reality behind the scenes. The actual
  delivery follows the diagram.

The Proven Process does double duty. In a sales conversation, it functions
as a confidence builder — prospects who have been burned by vendors
without a process are reassured when they see one they can point at and
ask questions about. Internally, it becomes one of the documented core
processes under Tool 6 (Three-Step Process Documenter™). The
customer-facing Proven Process on the V/TO and the internal core process
for customer delivery are the same object viewed from two angles, and they
should be linked rather than duplicated.

### 4. Guarantee — a promise that removes risk

The fourth sub-part is a concrete, specific promise that removes the
biggest perceived buying risk in the category. A real guarantee has two
parts: a clear promise and a tangible consequence if the promise isn't
met. "We guarantee satisfaction" is not a guarantee — it is a marketing
phrase. A usable guarantee specifies what will happen, by when, and what
the customer gets if it doesn't.

Not every business carries a guarantee. The book notes that roughly half
of the companies that build a V/TO ultimately choose not to have one,
usually because the category genuinely does not support it or the biggest
buying risk cannot be credibly absorbed by the vendor. In those cases the
team should make the decision consciously and record it as a deliberate
non-guarantee, not leave the field blank.

When a guarantee can be crafted credibly, it has two compounding effects:
it closes more business by removing the buyer's single biggest objection,
and it forces the internal discipline needed to actually honor the
promise. A team that commits to a guarantee has to get very good at
delivering on it, which in practice raises the quality of the whole
operation.

## How Marketing Strategy connects to the rest of the system

Marketing Strategy is not a standalone section. It feeds and is fed by
several other tools:

- **Core Focus** defines the sweet spot; Marketing Strategy names the
  customers inside that sweet spot and the way the company serves them.
  A Marketing Strategy that points outside the Core Focus is a warning
  sign that the team has not actually committed to the sweet spot.
- **Proven Process** is itself one of the core processes documented
  under Tool 6. The two should share the same name, the same steps, and
  the same owner. Documentation effort is not duplicated — the V/TO
  names the process and shows it at a high level, and the Core Process
  document in Tool 6 captures the full working version.
- **Target Market** drives the Scorecard™. Lead generation metrics
  (weekly leads, qualified prospects, first meetings booked) should
  measure activity aimed at the named target market, not generic
  outreach. If the Scorecard is tracking leads that do not match the
  target market, either the Scorecard or the Marketing Strategy is
  wrong — and usually it is the Scorecard chasing volume at the expense
  of fit.
- **Three Uniques** become part of the sales story that every
  leadership team member and every salesperson tells. They should
  appear on the company website, in sales decks, and in the language
  the team uses to describe the company in meetings.
- **Guarantee** becomes an operational constraint. The processes and
  Scorecard numbers that support guarantee delivery need to exist, and
  the Integrator is responsible for making sure the promise is actually
  keep-able.

## Substance criteria

A Marketing Strategy passes the substance check when:

- All four sub-parts are present on the V/TO — none omitted, none
  collapsed into another
- **Target Market** is filterable enough that a salesperson could
  build a real prospect list from it in an afternoon (specific
  industry, size, role, geography, or situation)
- **Three Uniques** contain exactly three entries, each specific
  enough that the combination is defensible against every real
  competitor the team can name
- **Proven Process** has a real name and a simple visual of its major
  steps, and it matches the customer-delivery core process documented
  under Tool 6
- **Guarantee** is either a concrete promise with a tangible
  consequence, or a consciously recorded decision not to carry one
- Everything in the Marketing Strategy points inside the Core Focus
  sweet spot, not outside it

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague target market.** "Small businesses" or "companies that need
  our solution" — the description fails the filter test and cannot
  drive real prospecting.
- **Generic Uniques.** Differentiators every competitor could honestly
  claim ("experienced team," "great service"). The combination has no
  defensive value.
- **Wrong count of Uniques.** Listing two, four, or five instead of
  exactly three. The number is deliberate — the discipline of picking
  exactly three is part of the forcing function.
- **Unnamed Proven Process.** A process the team uses internally but
  has never named, diagrammed, or shown to a prospect. Doesn't do the
  sales-confidence work that justifies the section.
- **Weak or weasel-worded Guarantee.** "Satisfaction guaranteed"
  without any defined consequence. The customer cannot invoke it, so
  it is not a guarantee.
- **Marketing Strategy that points outside Core Focus.** Target
  Market, Uniques, or Proven Process that describe work the company
  is not actually exceptional at. Usually a sign that Core Focus was
  not taken seriously.
- **Disconnection from the Scorecard.** Lead generation metrics
  measuring generic volume instead of target-market-specific
  activity.
- **Duplicated Proven Process.** Naming the customer process on the
  V/TO and then separately documenting a different version of the
  same process under Tool 6. The two should be one object.

## When to apply

- **Phase 2 V/TO session**, as the answer to question 4 of the eight
  V/TO questions, after Core Focus has been locked in
- **Annual Planning Session**, as part of the Vision page walkthrough —
  confirm the target market, uniques, process, and guarantee still
  describe reality
- **Whenever the sales pipeline is struggling**, as a diagnostic — an
  underperforming pipeline is often a symptom of a weak Marketing
  Strategy rather than a weak sales team
- **Whenever the company is debating a new segment or a new offering**
  — test the proposed move against the existing Marketing Strategy
  before committing
- **Whenever the Core Processes under Tool 6 are being rewritten** —
  the customer-delivery core process and the V/TO Proven Process must
  stay synchronized
