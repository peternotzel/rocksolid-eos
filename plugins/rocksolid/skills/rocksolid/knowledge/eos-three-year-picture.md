# 3-Year Picture

The 3-Year Picture™ is the answer to question 5 on the V/TO™
(Vision/Traction Organizer™), the fourth tool installed by the
Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™). It is the only forward-looking
element on the Vision page, and it occupies the entire right column of
that page on its own. Everything in the left column of the Vision page —
Core Values, Core Focus™, 10-Year Target™, Marketing Strategy — describes
the stable identity of the company, the parts that should change slowly
if at all. The right column is where the leadership team gets to describe
a concrete, vivid snapshot of what the business will have become three
years from now: how big, how profitable, how it feels, how it operates,
what it has built. The section is deliberately more emotional than the
rest of the Vision page, because its job is to give every leader in the
room something they can picture and want.

## Why three years, specifically

The three-year horizon is chosen deliberately and sits in a particular
spot in the vision-to-traction cascade:

- **Long enough to require real strategy.** Most teams can imagine a
  version of the business twelve months out without changing much. Three
  years is long enough that the team has to think about what the company
  will have built, hired, and learned, not just what it will have
  executed on.
- **Short enough to be vivid.** Unlike the 10-Year Target, which is a
  single long-range goal, the 3-Year Picture is meant to be picturable.
  Three years is close enough that the team can actually imagine the
  office, the team, the product, the customers — the things that go
  fuzzy at ten years out.
- **The middle link in the cascade.** The 10-Year Target sits above it;
  the 1-Year Plan sits below it. Each level is derived from the level
  above. The 3-Year Picture is the bridge that lets the annual plan
  connect to the long target without either collapsing into the other.

The 3-Year Picture is refreshed once a year, at the Annual Planning
Session, so it rolls forward one year at a time. At that session the
team looks back at the previous year's picture, evaluates what has
actually happened, and re-draws the picture for the new three-year
horizon.

## Required fields

The 3-Year Picture is not a free-form section — it has specific required
fields, and every one of them has to be filled in before the picture is
considered complete.

### Future date

A specific calendar or fiscal year-end date, three years out from the
current V/TO. The date is concrete, not "sometime in 2029" — it is
chosen to line up with the team's planning calendar so that everyone in
the room is imagining the same moment. The convention is a year-end
because year-ends are the moments most teams naturally think in.

### Revenue target

The revenue the business will generate in the twelve months leading up
to the future date. A single number, chosen after honest debate rather
than by multiplying the current run rate by an optimistic growth rate.
The number has to be something every leader in the room believes is
both meaningful (not a rounding error above today) and plausible (not a
fantasy that no one on the team actually thinks is reachable).

### Profit target

The profit the business will generate at that revenue level, stated
consistently (net margin, EBITDA, or whichever metric the team tracks
internally). This number forces the team to think about not just top-line
growth but how the company will be making money at that scale. Teams
that fill in a big revenue number and leave the profit number vague are
planning growth without planning economics, which usually ends badly.

### Measurables

Additional KPIs beyond revenue and profit that signal scale in this
specific business. Which measurables belong here varies by business —
examples of the kinds of numbers that fit include:

- Unit counts (customers, users, accounts, subscribers, transactions)
- Headcount (total employees, or specific team sizes)
- Location counts (offices, stores, regions, markets)
- Capacity numbers (production throughput, service volume, coverage)
- Product or service mix (percentage from a new line, percentage from
  recurring revenue, percentage in a particular segment)

The point is not to fill in every possible metric but to include the
two or three numbers beyond revenue and profit that would tell the team
whether the three-year version of the company has actually been built.

### "What does it look like?" bullets

The most distinctive part of the 3-Year Picture is a bullet list
describing what the business will feel like, look like, and operate like
at the future date. This is the section where the emotional pull lives.
The book recommends roughly ten to twenty bullets, though the count is
less important than the quality.

Good "looks like" bullets are vivid enough that every leader in the
room can mentally see the thing being described, and concrete enough to
guide real decisions. They cover things like:

- People — size of the team, quality of the team, specific roles filled
- Physical and operational footprint — offices, facilities, systems
- Operational maturity — which processes are running cleanly, which
  parts of the business no longer require founder attention
- Client or customer mix — the shape of the customer base, the type of
  work being won
- Product or service mix — what the company is selling, what has been
  launched, what has been retired
- Market position — how the company is perceived in its segment
- Culture and rhythm — how it feels to work there, how it feels to
  deliver for customers

What the bullets are NOT is poetry. Phrases like "world-class
experience" or "industry-leading quality" are vision-fluff and do not
belong on the 3-Year Picture. A good bullet is specific enough that the
team could check, three years from now, whether it came true.

## Position in the vision-to-traction cascade

The 3-Year Picture is the middle link in a four-level cascade:

```
10-Year Target → 3-Year Picture → 1-Year Plan → Quarterly Rocks
```

Each level is derivable from the level above it. The 3-Year Picture
should describe a version of the company that is on track to reach the
10-Year Target. The 1-Year Plan should describe a year that puts the
company on track to reach the 3-Year Picture. The Quarterly Rocks
should describe a quarter that delivers on the 1-Year Plan. When the
team walks the cascade end-to-end and any level does not trace cleanly
to the level above, one of the two is wrong.

This traceability is the main reason the 3-Year Picture has to be
concrete. An abstract forward picture ("become a leader in our space")
cannot generate a concrete 1-Year Plan, and an abstract 1-Year Plan
cannot generate concrete Quarterly Rocks. Fuzziness at the top of the
cascade cascades downward and eventually produces execution drift.

## Cadence — refreshed annually, not quarterly

The 3-Year Picture lives on the slow-changing side of the V/TO, but it
is the element on the Vision page that changes most often: once a year,
at the Annual Planning Session. At that session the team:

- Looks at last year's 3-Year Picture and evaluates which bullets came
  true, which did not, and which turned out to be the wrong things to
  chase
- Rolls the horizon forward one year
- Updates revenue, profit, and measurables for the new horizon
- Rewrites the "looks like" bullets as a new honest description of the
  business three years from the new planning date

What the team does NOT do is touch the 3-Year Picture quarterly. The
Quarterly Planning Session refreshes the Traction page; the Vision page
stays as it was set at the last annual session. Re-litigating the
3-Year Picture every ninety days defeats the purpose of having a
stable forward horizon.

## Substance criteria

A 3-Year Picture passes the substance check when:

- A specific future date is present (typically a year-end three years
  out, aligned with the team's planning calendar)
- A revenue target is present at that future date, chosen by honest
  debate rather than by rote growth multiplication
- A profit target is present, stated consistently (margin, EBITDA, or
  whichever metric the team uses internally)
- At least two or three measurables beyond revenue and profit are
  present, chosen because they signal scale in this business
- At least several "looks like" bullets are present, vivid enough to
  picture and concrete enough to check, not poetry or vision-fluff
- The picture traces upward — it describes a company on the path to
  the 10-Year Target
- The picture traces downward — a 1-Year Plan can be derived from it
- Every leader in the room can mentally visualize it and commit to it
  before the team moves on to question 6

## Common pitfalls

- **Missing financial fields.** Filling in "looks like" bullets and
  leaving revenue or profit blank. The numbers are required, and
  leaving them blank usually signals that the team has not done the
  work of debating what kind of business it actually wants in three
  years.
- **No "looks like" bullets.** Filling in revenue, profit, and
  measurables but skipping the bullet description. Loses the emotional
  pull and produces a 3-Year Picture no one in the room actually
  wants.
- **Generic "looks like" bullets.** Vision-fluff phrasing
  ("world-class team," "amazing culture," "industry leader") that
  cannot be checked or acted on.
- **Date that does not match the planning calendar.** Picking a random
  future date instead of a year-end lined up with Annual Planning.
  Makes the cascade hard to maintain because the horizon never rolls
  cleanly.
- **Breaking the cascade upward.** Writing a 3-Year Picture that is
  not on the path to the 10-Year Target, or that silently contradicts
  Core Focus. Usually a signal that one of the other Vision-page
  elements is not yet real for the team.
- **Breaking the cascade downward.** A 3-Year Picture abstract enough
  that no 1-Year Plan can be derived from it. Produces a stuck Annual
  Planning session where the team cannot figure out what the year
  should look like.
- **Re-litigating every quarter.** Touching the 3-Year Picture at
  every Quarterly Planning Session instead of leaving it stable
  between annuals. Defeats the purpose of having a slow-changing
  forward horizon.
- **Numbers no one believes.** A revenue target the CFO knows is
  impossible and the salespeople privately ignore. Erodes the
  credibility of the whole V/TO.

## When to apply

- **Phase 2 V/TO session**, as the answer to question 5 of the eight
  V/TO questions, after the 10-Year Target has been set
- **Annual Planning Session**, to roll the horizon forward one year
  and rebuild the picture for the new three-year window
- **Whenever the 10-Year Target is rolled down** (because the company
  has reached within three years of it and a fresh long target is
  being set) — the 3-Year Picture becomes the bridge between the old
  and new targets
- **Whenever the business changes fundamentally** — acquisition,
  major pivot, leadership change — the picture may need to be
  rewritten out-of-cycle
