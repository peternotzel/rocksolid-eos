# Core Focus

Core Focus™ is the answer to the second question on the V/TO™
(Vision/Traction Organizer™) and one of the most under-appreciated
disciplines in the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™). On the page
it occupies only a few lines, but those lines do load-bearing strategic
work: they name the deeper reason the organization exists and the single
thing it is genuinely exceptional at, and together they define the
boundary inside which the company is allowed to play. Once Core Focus is
written, the discipline becomes saying no to opportunities that fall
outside the boundary, even attractive ones — because growth outside the
sweet spot is the most reliable way for an otherwise-healthy company to
lose its identity, dilute its execution, and accumulate the kinds of
problems that look like operational failures but are actually strategic
ones.

## The two sub-parts

Core Focus has exactly two sub-parts, and both must be present and
substantive on the V/TO. Neither one can be collapsed into the other,
and neither one can be left vague.

### Purpose / Cause / Passion — the WHY beyond money

The first sub-part is the reason the organization exists beyond making
money. The book uses three near-synonyms — Purpose, Cause, Passion — and
the team picks whichever word feels most natural; they all point at the
same idea. The label matters less than the underlying answer to: "Why
does this company exist? What would the team feel was lost if it shut
down tomorrow?"

The required properties of a real Purpose / Cause / Passion statement:

- **Stated in very few words.** A long paragraph is a sign the team is
  hedging. A real purpose collapses into a phrase.
- **Emotionally real.** The leadership team can read it out loud without
  embarrassment. If anyone winces, it isn't the actual purpose.
- **Broad enough to outlast a specific industry.** Stated in principle,
  the purpose could carry the company into a different industry without
  losing its meaning. This is one of the tests for distinguishing
  Purpose from Niche — the purpose is a deeper "why" that does not
  depend on the specific service or product offering.
- **Survives a recession.** If the team reads the statement during a
  bad quarter and still believes it, it is real. If the statement only
  works when revenue is up and to the right, it isn't a purpose — it's
  a celebration.

The Purpose / Cause / Passion is what gets people out of bed when the
work is hard and the results are slow. A company without one tends to
optimize for short-term financial returns, which works fine until the
first crisis arrives.

### Niche — the ONE thing the company does exceptionally well

The second sub-part is the single thing the company is genuinely
exceptional at doing today. Three words in that sentence are
load-bearing:

- **Single.** Not a list of capabilities, not a portfolio of strengths,
  not "we do these five things well." One thing.
- **Exceptional.** Not "good at," not "competent at," not "we can do."
  Exceptional means there is a defensible reason this company is
  better at this than the alternatives a customer might pick.
- **Today.** Not aspirational, not "where we're heading," not a future
  capability. Niche is descriptive of current reality. There must be
  evidence the company is already exceptional at it — actual customers,
  actual outcomes, actual reputation in the market.

The reason for the strictness is that aspirational niches drag the
company in directions it cannot actually deliver on. A team that names
a niche it isn't yet exceptional at will spend the next year chasing
work it can't fulfill, while neglecting the work it could have
dominated.

## Together they form the "sweet spot"

The combination of Purpose / Cause / Passion and Niche defines the
company's "sweet spot" — the zone inside which the team should
concentrate its energy. Visually, you can think of it as the
intersection of two circles: one circle is everything that aligns with
the deeper purpose, the other is everything that falls inside the
niche. The sweet spot is the overlap.

Inside the sweet spot, the company knows why it is doing the work
(purpose) and is exceptional at doing it (niche). The two together
produce the kind of compounding advantage that distinguishes a
focused company from a general-purpose competitor.

## The discipline — staying inside the sweet spot

Once the sweet spot is defined, Core Focus becomes a filter for every
new opportunity. The discipline is to decline anything that falls
outside it, even if the work is profitable in the short term.

This is the part most teams find hard. Saying no to a profitable
opportunity feels wasteful, and the case for taking the work always
sounds reasonable in the moment ("it's a one-time engagement," "the
client is willing to pay," "we have spare capacity," "it's only
slightly outside our normal scope"). The book is firm that these
opportunities are the primary failure mode for growing companies.

The reason is structural rather than moral. Every opportunity outside
the sweet spot:

- Pulls the leadership team's attention away from the work that
  actually compounds
- Forces the team to build capabilities that don't strengthen the
  niche
- Creates internal complexity that slows down everything else the
  company does
- Erodes the clarity of the company's identity in the market and in
  its own employees' minds
- Tends to be lower-margin than the team expects, because the company
  is not yet exceptional at it

A leadership team that says yes to enough off-sweet-spot work eventually
discovers it has become a different company than the one it intended to
be — and usually a worse one.

## Tests for whether Core Focus is real

The biggest risk with Core Focus is that the team writes down a
plausible-sounding answer that doesn't pass closer inspection. A few
tests to apply before committing the answer to the V/TO:

### Tests for Purpose / Cause / Passion

- **The recession test.** If revenue dropped fifty percent next
  quarter, would the team still believe the purpose statement? If the
  answer is "well, it depends on what we'd cut," the purpose is too
  closely tied to the current business model to be a real purpose.
- **The industry-portability test.** Could this purpose plausibly be
  carried into a different industry without losing its meaning? If
  the purpose only makes sense when applied to the current product
  category, it is being confused with the niche.
- **The read-aloud test.** Can the leadership team read the statement
  out loud in an all-hands meeting without anyone in the room
  cringing? If not, the statement is marketing copy, not purpose.

### Tests for Niche

- **The evidence test.** Is there visible, current evidence the
  company is exceptional at this — happy customers, repeat business,
  inbound referrals, strong outcomes? If the evidence is hypothetical
  ("we will be exceptional at this once we hire the right people"),
  it is wishful thinking, not a niche.
- **The single-statement test.** Can the niche be stated in one
  sentence, without "and" or commas linking multiple capabilities?
  If the statement requires a list, the team has not yet identified
  the actual niche — it has just enumerated a portfolio.
- **The decline test.** Has the team turned down work in the last
  twelve months because it fell outside the niche? If not, the niche
  is not yet operational as a filter.

## Common failure: confusing what you do with what you're great at

The most common failure mode in a Core Focus session is conflating the
list of services the company offers with the niche it is exceptional
at. These are not the same thing. A company might offer fifteen
services but only be exceptional at one of them. The niche is that
one — not the other fourteen.

The fix is to ask, on each candidate niche statement: "Is this what
we *do*, or is this what we are *exceptional* at?" The answers usually
diverge sharply once the question is asked plainly. The exceptional
work is what belongs on the V/TO; the rest is ordinary delivery that
many competitors could match.

A second related failure is naming the niche at the wrong altitude.
Too high and the niche becomes generic ("software for businesses").
Too low and it becomes a single product feature. The right altitude
is usually a specific kind of work for a specific kind of customer,
phrased in a sentence that a layperson could understand.

## Substance criteria

A Core Focus passes the substance check when:

- Both sub-parts are present on the V/TO — neither is omitted, neither
  is collapsed into the other
- The Purpose / Cause / Passion statement is brief (a phrase, not a
  paragraph), emotionally real, and survives the recession and
  industry-portability tests
- The Niche statement is a single sentence, names something the
  company is exceptional at *today* (not aspirationally), and is
  backed by visible evidence
- The sweet spot is operational — the team has actually said no to
  off-sweet-spot opportunities in the recent past, or is willing to
  commit to doing so going forward
- Neither sub-part is a generic abstraction that could apply to any
  competitor in the same category

## Common pitfalls

- **Collapsing both sub-parts into one.** Treating Core Focus as a
  single statement instead of two distinct elements. Both are
  required, and they answer different questions.
- **Aspirational niche.** Naming what the company hopes to be
  exceptional at instead of what it is exceptional at today. Drags
  the team into work it cannot deliver.
- **Listed niche.** Stating the niche as a portfolio ("we do A and B
  and C and D") instead of a single thing. The discipline of "one
  thing" is the entire point.
- **Marketing-copy purpose.** A statement that sounds good in a pitch
  deck but cannot survive the read-aloud test in an all-hands.
- **Confusing what you do with what you're great at.** Listing
  offerings instead of identifying the one offering that is
  exceptional.
- **Sweet spot that nobody enforces.** Writing Core Focus on the V/TO
  and then continuing to take whatever work walks in the door. The
  written answer is meaningless without the discipline.
- **Niche too high or too low.** A niche stated at the level of "a
  whole industry" or at the level of "a single feature" cannot be
  used as a strategic filter.

## When to apply

- **Phase 2 V/TO session**, as the answer to question 2 of the eight
  V/TO questions
- **Annual Planning Session**, as part of the Vision page walkthrough
  — confirm the sweet spot still describes reality
- **Whenever the leadership team is debating a major new opportunity**
  — apply Core Focus as the filter before the financial analysis
- **Whenever the team feels the company has lost its identity** —
  usually a signal that off-sweet-spot work has accumulated and the
  team needs to renegotiate what stays and what gets cut
