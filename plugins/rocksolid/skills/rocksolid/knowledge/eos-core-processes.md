# Core Processes

Core Processes are the small number of repeatable workflows that together
describe how a business actually operates. They are the subject of Tool 6
in the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™) — the Process Component —
and they are the sixth tool installed, coming after the People, Rocks,
Meeting Pulse™, Scorecard™, and V/TO™ work. The point of the Process
Component is not to invent new procedures. The processes already exist in
some form; they are running every day in the hands of the people doing
the work. The point is to make them explicit, give them names everyone
agrees on, assign them owners, document them at a useful level of
detail, and enforce consistent usage. Companies that complete this work
stop relying on individual memory and start relying on repeatable
systems, which is the precondition for scaling without losing quality.

## Why 5-15 processes, not 100 and not 3

The book is deliberate about the number: most businesses operate on
somewhere between five and fifteen core processes, regardless of
industry or size. The range is load-bearing at both ends.

- **Fewer than five** usually means the team has collapsed distinct
  workflows into a single vague bucket, or has not yet surfaced
  processes that exist implicitly. Teams that name three processes
  almost always discover more during the identification conversation.
- **More than fifteen** usually means the team has mistaken
  sub-procedures or checklists for core processes. Every process has
  sub-steps, and sub-steps have their own sub-steps; the discipline
  is to stop at the level of the workflows that describe how the
  business runs end-to-end, not at the level of every task a single
  role performs.

The sweet spot is the number of processes a leadership team could list
on one page, each with an owner and a one-line description, and have
that list actually describe the business. Fewer than that and work is
happening that isn't captured; more than that and the team has
document inflation, which ends in a binder nobody reads.

## Identify before documenting

The biggest failure mode in process work is skipping identification and
jumping straight to documentation. A team that says "let's document our
processes" and then assigns documentation tasks produces a pile of
procedures that often describe different things by different names,
overlap in confusing ways, and leave gaps between them. The identify
step exists to prevent exactly this.

Identification is a single leadership team conversation — typically at
least an hour — whose entire goal is to reach one hundred percent
agreement on the count of core processes and their names. No
documentation happens until the list is locked. The team usually
discovers three kinds of friction during this conversation:

- **Different names for the same process.** Two people describe the
  same workflow using different vocabulary. Identification forces a
  single name.
- **One name hiding two processes.** What the team has been calling
  "delivery" is actually two distinct workflows with different
  owners and different rhythms. Identification forces the split.
- **Processes nobody owns.** Work that gets done but has no clear
  home on the Accountability Chart™. Identification surfaces these
  gaps so the chart can be fixed.

The output of the identify step is a clean list of process names with
leadership-team consensus on every entry.

## Typical categories of core processes

Without reproducing a canonical ordered list, the kinds of processes a
typical business surfaces during identification include:

- A **people flow** — how the company finds, evaluates, hires,
  onboards, manages, reviews, and eventually exits employees. The
  life cycle of the people inside the company.
- A **demand generation flow** — how the company creates awareness
  and generates qualified interest in its target market. Where
  prospects come from before they become prospects.
- A **customer acquisition flow** — how qualified interest is
  converted into paying customers. The sales workflow from first
  conversation through signed agreement. Often the same object as
  the Proven Process™ element of Marketing Strategy on the V/TO.
- **One or more delivery flows** — how the product or service is
  actually produced and delivered to the customer. Simple businesses
  have a single delivery flow; complex ones may have several
  (project delivery, logistics, quality, ongoing service).
- A **money flow** — how cash moves in and out of the business.
  Billing, collections, payables, payroll, reporting. The accounting
  and finance workflow.
- A **retention flow** — how customers are cared for after initial
  delivery so that they stay, return, buy more, and refer. Often
  overlooked because it is not obviously revenue-generating in the
  short term, but usually the highest-leverage process in the
  business.

The actual named list is always specific to the company. Some
businesses split the delivery flow into several processes; some roll
marketing and sales into one; some add category-specific flows that do
not fit any of these buckets. The categories above are a starting
prompt for the identification conversation, not a template to copy.

## Leadership consensus on names

Once the leadership team settles on a name for each process, that name
is used consistently across the entire organization from then on. No
departmental variants, no legacy aliases, no "we call it X here and Y
over there." The discipline sounds aesthetic but is functionally
important:

- Mixed naming makes process documents impossible to find, because
  nobody knows which name to search for.
- Mixed naming makes training impossible, because new employees get
  different versions of the same process depending on whom they ask.
- Mixed naming makes enforcement impossible, because two people can
  argue about whether a rule applies to the process they are running
  by claiming their process has a different name.

The book is firm: one hundred percent consensus on names, one hundred
percent consistency in usage. The leadership team is responsible for
enforcing both across every document, every training, and every
conversation from the moment the list is locked.

## Ownership maps to the Accountability Chart

Every core process has exactly one owner, and the owner is a seat on
the Accountability Chart — not a person by name. Ownership by seat
means the process survives personnel changes: when the person in the
seat leaves, the new person in the seat inherits the process without
the document having to be rewritten.

A process without an owner is a red flag. Either the Accountability
Chart is missing a seat that should exist, or the process has been
scoped wrong and actually belongs to an existing seat that has not
claimed it. The work is to fix one of the two before moving on, not
to leave the process orphaned.

Because ownership depends on the Accountability Chart being solid,
Core Processes cannot be done before Tool 1 is in place. Teams that
try to document processes before the chart exists end up re-assigning
ownership multiple times as the chart settles, which wastes most of
the documentation effort.

## Why Process lands sixth in the implementation order

Process work is deferred until late in the implementation sequence for
several reinforcing reasons:

- **Ownership depends on the Accountability Chart**, which is Tool 1.
- **The Proven Process element of the V/TO is itself a core process**,
  so the V/TO (Tool 5) has to exist first to make the customer-facing
  process explicit.
- **Without the weekly L10™ meeting and a running Scorecard**, process
  documents get filed away and ignored. Operating discipline is what
  enforces them.
- **Process work is operational rather than strategic.** It naturally
  comes after strategic clarity is in place, not before.
- **Process documentation is the most time-consuming activity of the
  core tools** — typically six to twelve months to complete across a
  full business, which is another reason to start it only once the
  team has earned the capacity.

Teams that attempt Core Processes in Phase 1 almost always produce
work they have to redo once the foundational tools catch up.

## Connection to Marketing Strategy's Proven Process

The Proven Process element of Marketing Strategy on the V/TO is not a
separate artifact from the Core Processes under Tool 6. The
customer-facing process that the sales team shows prospects and the
internally documented delivery process are the same object viewed
from two angles:

- The V/TO Proven Process shows the process at a high level — a
  simple named diagram a prospect can understand in under a minute.
- The Tool 6 core process documents the same workflow at the 20/80
  level — the major steps the team follows on every engagement.

Both should have the same name, the same owner, and the same overall
shape. Teams that accidentally let them drift apart end up with a
sales story that does not match delivery, which erodes customer
trust. The skill should enforce that the two are linked and
synchronized.

## Substance criteria

A Core Processes effort passes the substance check when:

- A named list of core processes exists and contains roughly five to
  fifteen entries
- The leadership team has explicitly reached one hundred percent
  consensus on the count and the names
- Every document in the organization refers to each process by the
  agreed name — no legacy aliases, no departmental variants
- Each process has exactly one owner, and the owner is a seat on the
  Accountability Chart (not a person by name, not "shared")
- The Proven Process element of Marketing Strategy is linked to the
  corresponding customer-facing core process — same name, same
  shape, same owner
- No process in the list is orphaned, and no observable workflow in
  the business is missing from the list
- Each process has been (or will be) documented at the 20/80 level
  following Tool 6's Three-Step Process Documenter™

## Common pitfalls

- **Skipping the identify step.** Jumping to documentation without
  agreeing on the list first. Produces incoherent output that has to
  be reorganized later.
- **Wrong count.** Three processes (too few, hiding real workflows)
  or fifty processes (too many, mistaking sub-procedures for core
  processes).
- **Different names for the same process.** Sales calls it one
  thing, delivery calls it another, training uses a third name.
  Undermines every downstream use of the process.
- **Unowned processes.** Entries on the list with no owner, or with
  "shared" ownership by multiple seats. One seat, always.
- **Owner named by person instead of seat.** Breaks the moment that
  person moves to a different role.
- **Disconnected from the V/TO.** The Proven Process on the Vision
  page and the core process under Tool 6 drift apart or are treated
  as separate things.
- **Starting before the foundational tools are in place.** Process
  work done before the Accountability Chart and the V/TO exist has
  to be redone, usually from scratch.

## When to apply

- **Phase 3 kickoff**, as the sixth foundational tool, after People,
  Rocks, Meeting Pulse, Scorecard, and V/TO are in place
- **Whenever the Accountability Chart is restructured** — verify
  that every process still has an owner on the new chart
- **Whenever the V/TO Marketing Strategy is updated** — confirm the
  Proven Process is still linked to its corresponding core process
- **Whenever a process-related issue surfaces in the weekly L10** —
  use the issue to test whether the core process is named, owned,
  and documented, and to surface whichever of the three is missing
