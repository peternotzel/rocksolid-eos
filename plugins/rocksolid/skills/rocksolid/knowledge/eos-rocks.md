# Rocks

Rocks™ are the second tool the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™)
installs after the Accountability Chart™, and they convert fresh clarity of
ownership into ninety days of concrete execution. A Rock is a priority — not
a task, not an open-ended initiative, not a vague aspiration — that the owner
commits to completing within a single quarter. The discipline is deliberately
narrowing: each leader is allowed only a handful of Rocks per quarter, and
each company itself is allowed only a handful of company-level Rocks. The
purpose of that ceiling is not laziness but the opposite. When everything is
called a priority, nothing actually is, and the team's attention scatters
across so many fronts that no front advances. Rocks force the leadership team
to choose, in writing, the small set of things that must be true at the end
of ninety days for the business to have moved.

## What a Rock is (and is not)

A Rock is a quarterly priority with a clear destination, a single owner, and
a binary scoring rule at quarter-end. It is not the work itself — it is the
outcome the work is supposed to produce. It is not a task on a to-do list,
because tasks are seven-day items, not ninety-day commitments. It is not a
KPI either, because a KPI is a recurring weekly number tracked on the
Scorecard™ rather than a finite project. A Rock is the kind of thing a
leader, when asked at the end of the quarter "did you do what you said you
would do," can answer with a clean yes or a clean no.

## The 3-7 rule across tiers

Wickman fixes the count of Rocks at three tiers in the organization:

- **Company Rocks per quarter:** between three and seven, with the lower
  end of that range strongly preferred.
- **Individual Rocks per leadership team member:** between three and seven
  total, *inclusive* of any company Rocks the member personally owns. It is
  not three to seven on top of company Rocks — it is three to seven all in.
- **Rocks per non-leadership employee:** between one and three.

The principle behind all three numbers is the same: focus produces traction,
overload produces drift. A leader carrying ten Rocks completes none of them
well. A frontline employee carrying five Rocks loses sight of the day job.

## The SMA criteria

Every Rock must clear three filters before it goes onto the Rock Sheet:

- **Specific** — the destination can be described in one plain sentence.
  Anyone reading the Rock should be able to picture what "done" looks like
  without asking follow-up questions.
- **Measurable** — there is a concrete artifact or number attached. "Improve
  customer onboarding" is not measurable. "Onboarding playbook v1 published
  and trained to the CS team" is.
- **Attainable** — the work can plausibly fit into ninety days alongside the
  owner's day job. Rocks that would take six months are not Rocks; they are
  initiatives that need to be sliced into a ninety-day first cut.

At quarter-end the Rock is scored binary: done or not done. There is no
partial credit, no "we got most of the way there," no fractional weighting.
The reason for the harshness is that any softer rule lets teams rationalize
their way into believing they performed better than they actually did, and
the integrity of the discipline collapses within a couple of quarters.

## The single-owner rule

Each Rock has exactly one named owner. Not two co-owners. Not a team. Not a
department. One human being whose name sits next to the Rock on the Rock
Sheet and whose reputation is on the line at the next quarterly review.

The reasoning is operational rather than philosophical. When two leaders
share ownership of a priority, each one assumes on some level that the other
will pick up the ball when things get hard, and the work stalls in the gap
between them. A single named owner closes that gap. The owner does not
necessarily do all the work — they may delegate substantial pieces — but
they are the one who schedules it, drives it forward, removes blockers, and
stands in front of the team on reporting day.

## The 8-step Rock-setting process

Wickman prescribes an explicit eight-step process for setting the quarter's
Rocks. It is run by the leadership team in a working session, ideally as
part of a Quarterly Planning Session.

1. **Brainstorm everything.** With the V/TO™ in front of them (or, in
   Phase 1 of this skill, with rough directional intuition in place of the
   V/TO), the leadership team dumps every candidate priority onto a
   whiteboard. A typical first pass produces ten to twenty candidates.
2. **Narrow by keep, kill, or combine.** The team debates each candidate
   and decides whether to carry it forward, drop it, or merge it into
   another. Multiple passes are taken until the list is reduced to between
   three and seven company Rocks.
3. **Set the due date and sharpen.** Each surviving Rock is rewritten until
   it is unambiguously specific, measurable, and attainable. The due date
   is almost always the final calendar day of the quarter.
4. **Assign exactly one owner.** Each company Rock is given to one and only
   one leadership team member.
5. **Set individual Rocks.** Each leader carries forward any company Rocks
   they personally own, then adds their own priorities until their
   individual list reaches three to seven total. Candidates that were
   dropped from the company list in Step 2 often reappear here as
   individual Rocks. Overflow ideas that still don't make the cut go onto
   the V/TO Issues List for future quarters.
6. **Build the Rock Sheet.** A single landscape document is created with
   company Rocks at the top and each leader's individual Rocks below.
7. **Share with the organization.** The company Rocks (not the individual
   ones) are presented at a state-of-the-company meeting capped at
   forty-five minutes, with the entire workforce invited.
8. **Cascade to departments.** Each department runs the same eight-step
   process with its own team, ending with every employee holding one to
   three Rocks for the quarter.

## The Rock Sheet — one artifact, not two

A structurally important detail for the skill: the Rock Sheet is a single
landscape-format document. Company Rocks occupy the top region of the page
and each leadership member's individual Rocks appear in blocks below them on
the same page. It is not two separate templates. The reason is rhythm —
during the weekly Level 10™ Meeting (L10™), the Rock Review segment walks
the sheet top to bottom in five minutes, which is only possible if it is
literally one sheet. Two separate files break the cadence and make the
weekly review awkward.

## Less is more

The phrase Wickman returns to again and again on Rocks is that focus beats
activity. A team that completes four Rocks brilliantly produces more
business value than a team that half-completes nine. Every quarter the
leadership team will feel pressure to add an eighth or ninth Rock because
"this matters too." That pressure must be resisted. Anything important that
does not fit goes onto the Issues List for next quarter's Rock-setting,
not onto this quarter's Rock Sheet.

## Cascade to the department level

Once the leadership team has set company and individual Rocks, the same
eight-step process runs at each department's level. Department heads
brainstorm, narrow, sharpen, assign owners, and produce a department Rock
Sheet. Frontline employees end up with one to three Rocks each. The cascade
is what makes the quarterly priorities visible all the way down to the seat
level, and it is the structural mechanism that connects the company's
ninety-day focus to the day-to-day work of every employee.

## State-of-the-company sharing

After the leadership team sets the quarter's Rocks, the company Rocks are
shared with the entire organization in a meeting capped at forty-five
minutes. The meeting also walks recent wins and the V/TO headlines. The
purpose is not ceremony — it is repetition. People do not internalize a
priority the first time they hear it stated, and the state-of-the-company
forum is one of the main places where leaders repeat themselves until the
quarterly priorities become part of the shared vocabulary.

## Phase 1 Rocks before the V/TO

A common objection is that you cannot set Rocks until you have built a
V/TO, since V/TO is the tool that defines where the company is going.
Wickman's practical answer is that most leadership teams already have a
rough directional sense of where they are heading, and that intuition is
enough to set a first pass at Rocks. The discipline of running the Rock
cycle itself exposes gaps in the team's shared understanding, which makes
the eventual V/TO work more grounded. For this skill, that means Phase 1
"pre-vision" Rocks are legitimate — but they should be reviewed and
realigned through a Rock Alignment Check once the V/TO is built in Phase 2.

## Criteria / Rules

**Hard rules (must pass):**

- Between three and seven company Rocks for the quarter.
- Between three and seven individual Rocks per leadership team member,
  inclusive of company Rocks they own.
- Between one and three Rocks per non-leadership employee.
- Each Rock is specific, measurable, and attainable.
- Each Rock has exactly one named owner — never shared, never a team.
- Each Rock has an explicit due date, almost always the final calendar
  day of the quarter.
- Each Rock can be scored binary at quarter-end: done or not done.
- A single Rock Sheet artifact exists in landscape format covering both
  company Rocks and all individual Rocks on one page.
- The company Rocks have been presented at a state-of-the-company meeting
  within the quarter.

**Soft rules (context-dependent):**

- The lower end of the 3-7 range (closer to three) is preferred.
- First-time Rock-setters complete around half their Rocks; calibration
  improves over two or three quarters.
- Pre-vision Phase 1 Rocks are accepted but get a forward realignment
  check after the V/TO exists.

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague Rocks.** "Improve customer service" is not a Rock. A Rock must
  be specific enough that done versus not done can be stated without
  argument.
- **Too many Rocks.** Eight or nine company Rocks instead of three to
  seven dilutes focus and predicts a low completion rate.
- **No owner or shared owners.** Violates the single-owner rule and
  predicts the work will stall.
- **Mid-quarter additions.** New ideas that surface mid-quarter must go
  to the Issues List for next quarter's consideration, not onto the
  current Rock Sheet.
- **Tasks dressed up as Rocks.** A Rock that fits in a single afternoon
  is a to-do, not a quarterly priority.
- **Skipping the state-of-the-company share.** Without it, the
  quarterly priorities stay trapped inside the leadership team and the
  cascade never reaches the rest of the organization.
- **Expecting mastery on the first attempt.** First-time teams typically
  complete around half their Rocks; that is normal, not a sign the tool
  is broken.

## When to apply

- Phase 1, immediately after the Accountability Chart is in place.
- Every Quarterly Planning Session, as the centerpiece exercise.
- Phase 2 Rock Alignment Check, immediately after the V/TO is built.
