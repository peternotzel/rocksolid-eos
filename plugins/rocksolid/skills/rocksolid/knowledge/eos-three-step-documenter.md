# The Three-Step Process Documenter

The Three-Step Process Documenter™ is the method used to carry out Tool 6
of the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™) — the Process Component —
and it is how the leadership team turns the Core Processes from implicit
habit into explicit, owned, enforced systems. The tool is named for its
three stages: Identify, Document, and Compile. The order is fixed, and
skipping or collapsing any stage produces a predictable failure mode.
The Documenter exists because most companies already run on repeatable
patterns, but those patterns live inside individual heads and drift
quietly over time as people leave, new hires improvise, and the founder
accumulates too many "just ask me" bottlenecks. Running the Documenter
replaces "ask someone who remembers" with "look at the process," which
is the precondition for scaling the business without losing the quality
that made it work in the first place.

## The three stages

### Stage 1 — Identify

The first stage is a single leadership team conversation, typically at
least an hour long, whose entire goal is to reach one hundred percent
agreement on the list of core processes and their names. No
documentation happens until this list is locked.

The rules of the identify step:

- **The whole leadership team is in the room.** Not delegated to a
  single person, not a sub-committee, not an email thread. The
  conversation has to happen live because it surfaces disagreements
  about what counts as a process, what the names should be, and who
  owns what.
- **The goal is consensus on the count and the names.** Not general
  agreement, not majority rule — full agreement. A single leader
  objecting to a name or a boundary means the conversation continues.
- **No documentation starts until the list is locked.** Teams that
  start documenting before identification produce overlapping,
  inconsistently-named, gappy output that has to be reorganized
  later anyway.

The book is explicit that skipping this stage is the single most
common reason process efforts fail. Teams that "just start writing"
end up with a pile of procedures that don't fit together, and then
have to do identification afterward at much higher cost.

### Stage 2 — Document each process

Once the list is locked, each core process is documented by its
owner. Ownership follows the Accountability Chart™: whoever owns the
seat that runs a process is responsible for documenting it. This
assignment is deliberate and load-bearing:

- **Owners, not temps, do the work.** Documentation is not delegated
  to an intern, an outside consultant, or "whoever has bandwidth."
  The person whose seat owns the process is the one who writes it,
  because they are the one who knows how the work actually happens
  and who will be enforcing the process afterward.
- **The Integrator owns the overall project.** While individual
  leaders own individual processes, the Integrator seat is
  responsible for the whole documentation effort staying on track,
  for enforcing the format, and for making sure every process gets
  written within the target timeline.
- **Documentation is not a one-week sprint.** Most companies need
  six to twelve months to document the full set of core processes.
  The Integrator's job is to keep the effort moving without letting
  it stall.

The output at the end of this stage is a complete set of process
documents, one per core process, all following the same format.

### Stage 3 — Compile and distribute

The third stage is the moment the work becomes operational. The
individual documents are bundled into a single package — a physical
binder, an intranet folder, a wiki section, a shared drive — whose
medium does not matter but whose existence does. The process names
become the table of contents. The package is then distributed to
everyone who touches the processes, and the leadership team trains
the organization on the new documents.

From the moment of compile-and-distribute onward, the processes are
enforced. "The way we do it" becomes a real constraint, not a
suggestion. Deviations get surfaced as issues in the weekly L10™
meeting and addressed through IDS™. The Scorecard™ measures whether
the processes are being followed.

The compile step is what distinguishes a process effort that sticks
from one that evaporates. Teams that document processes but never
bundle and train on them usually discover, a year later, that the
documents exist on a shared drive nobody has opened.

## The 20/80 rule

The central discipline of the Documenter is the 20/80 rule: document
the twenty percent of the content that drives eighty percent of the
results, not the hundred percent that would drive a hundred percent
of theoretical coverage. This rule exists because teams that try to
document everything produce binders nobody reads, and binders nobody
reads enforce nothing.

### The 2-10 page sweet spot

Each documented process should land in a sweet spot of roughly two to
ten pages. Under two pages is under-documentation; over ten pages is
over-documentation. Operations processes tend to sit on the longer
end of the range; people and money flows tend to sit on the shorter
end.

### Over-documentation symptoms

Symptoms that a process has been over-documented include:

- **Twenty-plus page documents** for what should be a single
  workflow. Usually means the author has included every edge case,
  every exception, and every screen-by-screen detail.
- **Keystroke-level instructions** ("click the blue button in the
  top right, then select the third option from the dropdown"). This
  level of detail belongs in a checklist or a screenshot walkthrough,
  not in a core process document.
- **Language that tries to leave no judgment to the executor.** The
  goal of the document is to make sure the structural steps are
  followed, not to eliminate human decision-making. Over-documented
  processes read like legal contracts and slow the work down.
- **Sub-procedures embedded inline instead of linked.** If a step
  has its own multi-page procedure, the procedure should be its own
  reference document and the core process should link to it.

### Under-documentation symptoms

Symptoms that a process has been under-documented include:

- **A title list with no substance.** A one-page document that
  enumerates step names without explaining what happens at each
  step. Transfers no working knowledge.
- **Single-page summaries that miss the sub-steps.** The major steps
  are listed but the bullet details under each step are absent, so
  a new person following the document cannot actually run the
  process.
- **Missing who-does-what.** The document describes what happens but
  not who is responsible for each step, leaving ownership ambiguous.
- **Missing the triggers.** The document describes the steps but
  not what starts and ends the process, so a reader cannot tell
  when to run it.

## The structural format

Every core process document follows the same format:

- **Numbered major steps** (typically somewhere between three and
  ten per process)
- **Bullet sub-steps under each major step** (typically a handful
  per major step), describing the concrete actions inside the step

What the format is NOT:

- Not a flowchart or swimlane diagram. Visual process notation is
  harder to read quickly, harder to maintain, and harder to update
  than numbered text.
- Not procedural code or pseudo-code. The documents are read by
  humans, not machines.
- Not keystroke-level instructions. Those belong in separate
  checklists or reference guides.
- Not a free-form narrative. Narratives obscure structure.

The format is deliberately boring. Boring formats are easy to
produce, easy to read, easy to maintain, and easy to enforce.

## A recurring side effect: surfacing dead weight

One of the useful side effects of documenting a process is
discovering steps that exist only for historical reasons — habits the
team has been preserving without remembering why. The principle at
work is that traditions without a live reason should be questioned
rather than preserved. Documentation is the moment to ask, for each
step, whether it is actually doing anything today or just consuming
effort because nobody has ever revisited it. Teams that take this
opportunity seriously usually remove several steps per process
during the Document stage. The simplified version of the process is
what gets compiled and distributed.

## The naming discipline

The naming discipline from the identify step carries through the
entire Documenter. The name the leadership team agreed on during
Stage 1 is the name used in Stage 2 (the document title), in Stage 3
(the table of contents), and in every subsequent conversation about
the process. No departmental variants, no legacy aliases, no "we
call it X here and Y over there."

The naming discipline is not aesthetic. It is how the team reduces
communication cost, makes process documents findable, and preserves
the enforceability of the process after compilation. Mixed naming
undermines every downstream use of the documentation.

## Substance criteria

A Three-Step Process Documenter effort passes the substance check
when:

- **Stage 1 completed** — the leadership team has reached one
  hundred percent consensus on the count and names of the core
  processes before any documentation began
- **Stage 2 completed** — each core process has been documented by
  the owner of the corresponding seat on the Accountability Chart,
  not by a temp or a consultant
- Each documented process is between roughly two and ten pages
- Each documented process uses the major-steps-plus-bullet-sub-steps
  format — no flowcharts, no keystroke-level detail, no narrative
- The Integrator seat has overseen the project and kept it moving
  within the six-to-twelve-month window
- Each process has been sanity-read by someone other than the
  author to confirm it matches how the work is actually done
- Dead-weight steps surfaced during documentation have been pruned
  rather than preserved
- **Stage 3 completed** — the documents have been compiled into a
  single accessible package, distributed to everyone who touches
  them, and trained on
- Process names are used consistently across the organization from
  the compile-and-distribute moment onward

## Common pitfalls

- **Skipping Stage 1.** Jumping straight to documentation without
  identification. Produces incoherent output that has to be
  reorganized.
- **Delegating Stage 2 to a temp.** Handing documentation to an
  intern, a consultant, or an ops person who is not the owner of
  the seat running the process. The documents end up describing
  what the temp thinks the process is rather than what it actually
  is.
- **Over-documenting.** Producing twenty-plus-page processes full
  of keystroke-level detail. Nobody reads the result, and the
  effort is wasted.
- **Under-documenting.** Producing one-page summaries that don't
  transfer working knowledge. The documents exist on paper but
  can't actually train anyone.
- **Wrong format.** Flowcharts, swimlane diagrams, or free-form
  narratives instead of numbered major steps with bullet
  sub-steps. The format is load-bearing — mixing formats across
  processes undermines the whole effort.
- **Preserving dead-weight steps.** Keeping historical habits in
  the documented process without ever questioning whether they
  still serve a purpose.
- **Skipping Stage 3.** Documenting processes but never compiling,
  distributing, or training on them. The documents exist
  somewhere but no one reads them and no one enforces them.
- **Mixed naming after compile.** Continuing to use legacy names
  or departmental variants after the leadership team has locked in
  the agreed names. Undermines enforceability.

## When to apply

- **Phase 3 kickoff**, as the method for executing Tool 6 after
  the foundational tools are in place
- **Whenever a new core process is added** to the list — run a
  mini-Documenter for the new process: Identify (with leadership
  team), Document (by the seat owner), Compile (add to the
  package and train on it)
- **Whenever an existing process has drifted materially** — rerun
  Stage 2 for that process only, sanity-check against current
  practice, and redistribute
- **During the Annual Planning Session** — review the compiled
  process package and flag any processes that need to be updated
  in the coming year
