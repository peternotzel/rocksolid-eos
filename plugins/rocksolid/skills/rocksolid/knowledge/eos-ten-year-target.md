# The 10-Year Target

The 10-Year Target™ is the answer to the third question on the V/TO™
(Vision/Traction Organizer™) and the top of the Vision-to-Traction
cascade in the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™). It is the long-
range destination that everything below it on the V/TO eventually has to
ladder up to: the 3-Year Picture™ describes how the company looks in the
middle of the journey to it, the 1-Year Plan describes what has to be
true twelve months out for the journey to still be on schedule, and the
quarterly Rocks™ describe the specific ninety-day moves that advance
the plan. If the 10-Year Target is vague or missing, the entire cascade
loses its anchor — every layer below has nothing to derive from.

## What the 10-Year Target is

The 10-Year Target is a single, specific, measurable, ambitious goal set
roughly ten years out from the date the team is writing it. The book's
convention is ten years; some teams use five years and some use as long
as twenty, but the principle holds at any horizon: it is one big number
attached to one specific date, far enough out to be ambitious and close
enough to feel real.

The required properties:

- **One entry, not a list.** The 10-Year Target is a single goal. Not
  three, not a portfolio, not "here are five things we want to be true
  by 2036." The discipline of choosing one is the entire point.
- **Specific.** Specific enough that a reader can tell whether the
  company has hit it or not, without follow-up questions. In practice
  this almost always means a concrete number is attached.
- **Measurable.** The number is observable. Someone can look at the
  business in ten years and produce an unambiguous "yes, we hit it" or
  "no, we did not."
- **Inspiring.** Big enough to stretch the leadership team, the
  organization, and the imagination. Not "what we'd hit anyway if we
  kept doing what we're doing" — something that requires real
  ambition to reach.
- **Dated.** Tied to a real calendar horizon — a specific year, not
  "eventually" or "someday." A 10-Year Target with no date is not a
  target.

## Why one entry, not a list

The temptation in a planning session is to name several long-range
goals — a revenue target, a geographic expansion target, a product
target, a headcount target, an industry-leadership target — because
each of them feels important and dropping any one of them feels like
loss. The book is firm: a list is not a target. A list is a wish list,
and a wish list does not focus the organization the way a single
target does.

The deeper reason is that the entire purpose of the 10-Year Target is
to provide a single shared destination that every employee, in every
function, can point at and orient around. If there are five targets,
there is no destination — there are five competing ones, and the
organization splinters its attention across them. If there is one
target, every conversation about strategy has the same anchor.

The other elements that the team would have wanted to name as
additional targets — geography, headcount, product, market position —
belong on the 3-Year Picture instead, where multiple bullets are
appropriate.

## Why ten years specifically

The horizon is not arbitrary. Ten years is chosen because it is long
enough to be aspirational but short enough to feel real:

- **Five years is too close.** Most leadership teams can imagine the
  company five years out as roughly an extrapolation of what they are
  doing now. A five-year goal does not stretch imagination enough to
  produce a real BHAG.
- **Twenty-five years is too far.** The leadership team that is in
  the room today is unlikely to all still be there in twenty-five
  years, the industry context is unpredictable that far out, and the
  goal stops feeling like a real commitment.
- **Ten years is the sweet spot.** Long enough that the team has to
  imagine a fundamentally different version of the company, short
  enough that today's leadership team can credibly say they will
  still be around to see whether it happened.

That said, some teams pick five or twenty depending on industry — the
book accepts this. The principle is that the horizon should be long
enough to require ambition and short enough to feel like a real
commitment by the people writing it down.

## Top of the Vision-to-Traction cascade

The 10-Year Target sits at the top of a four-level cascade:

1. **10-Year Target** — single specific destination
2. **3-Year Picture** — vivid picture of the company three years out,
   with date, revenue, profit, measurables, and "looks like" bullets
3. **1-Year Plan** — what has to be true twelve months out for the
   3-Year Picture to still be on track, including 3-7 annual goals
4. **Quarterly Rocks** — the 3-7 ninety-day priorities that advance
   the 1-Year Plan

Each layer is meant to be derivable from the layer above. A team that
has done the cascade properly can read a Rock on the Rock Sheet and
trace it upward through a 1-Year goal, into a 3-Year Picture bullet,
and ultimately to the 10-Year Target. If a Rock cannot be traced
upward, either the Rock doesn't belong (it isn't really moving the
company toward where it said it was going) or the cascade has a gap
above it.

The top of the cascade is the 10-Year Target. Without it, the cascade
has no apex and the entire derivation chain becomes self-referential.

## Rolling the target down

The book describes a rolling convention for what happens when the
company gets close to its 10-Year Target. Once the company is within
about three years of the dated horizon, the long-range target is
"rolled down" into the 3-Year Picture and a fresh 10-Year Target is
set. The rationale is that a target three years out is no longer a
strategic anchor — it is a tactical one. The strategic horizon should
always be roughly a decade ahead.

The implication for the skill is that the 10-Year Target is not a
permanent fixture. It rolls forward as the company progresses, and
the team should expect to set a new one roughly once a decade or
whenever the existing one rolls into the 3-Year Picture's horizon.

## Tests for whether a 10-Year Target is good

A few practical tests to apply before committing the answer to the
V/TO:

### The 30-second test

Could a new hire understand the 10-Year Target in thirty seconds and
explain it to a friend at dinner that night? If yes, the target is
clear enough. If the new hire would need a paragraph of context, the
target is too complicated or too vague.

### The ambiguity test

Two people who know the company should be able to read the 10-Year
Target and reach the same answer to "did we hit it?" If they can
disagree, the target is ambiguous and needs sharpening — usually by
adding a number or a clearer measurable.

### The number test

Almost every good 10-Year Target has a number in it. Revenue, customer
count, geographic markets, units shipped, valuation, headcount — the
specific dimension varies, but a number is usually present because
numbers are unambiguous and most other phrasings are not. A target
without a number is suspicious and probably needs one.

### The date test

The date has to be specific. "Ten years from now" is fine in
conversation, but the V/TO entry should name the actual year (and
ideally the actual date). "Eventually" or "in the long run" are not
dates and disqualify the target.

### The stretch test

The leadership team should look at the target and feel that it is
reachable but not certain. If the answer is "we'll definitely hit
that," the target is too modest — it isn't pulling the organization
forward. If the answer is "there's no plausible path to that," the
target is too ambitious — it doesn't motivate, it discourages.

## Visual placement on the V/TO

A structural detail from the V/TO layout: the 10-Year Target sits in
the left column of the Vision page, between Core Focus™ and Marketing
Strategy. It is rendered as a single line — visually, the form gives
it one slot, not multiple, which reinforces the "one entry, not a
list" rule at the level of the page itself. Templates the skill
produces should preserve this single-line structural treatment;
multiple-line or multi-bullet treatments invite the team to drift
back into making a list.

## Substance criteria

A 10-Year Target passes the substance check when:

- It is a single entry, not a list of multiple targets
- It includes a specific number (or an unambiguous measurable
  equivalent)
- It is dated to a specific year, ten years out from the current V/TO
  date (with five-year or twenty-year horizons accepted as
  alternatives if the team has a justified reason)
- It can be stated in a single sentence
- It is measurable in a way that two reviewers would reach the same
  yes-or-no answer in ten years
- It is ambitious enough to require real stretch but not so ambitious
  that the leadership team privately considers it impossible
- A new hire could understand it and repeat it in thirty seconds
- The 3-Year Picture, 1-Year Plan, and Quarterly Rocks below it ladder
  upward to it without contradiction

## Common pitfalls

- **Vague language.** "Become the best at X," "be the leader in our
  category," "transform the industry." None of these can be
  unambiguously verified in ten years.
- **Missing number.** A target with no measurable dimension cannot be
  scored. Add a number.
- **Missing date.** "Eventually" or "in the long run" are not dates.
  Pin a year.
- **Multiple targets disguised as one.** A sentence with three
  conjunctions is a list, not a target. Pick one and move the others
  to the 3-Year Picture.
- **Modest target.** "Grow at the same rate we've been growing" is
  not a 10-Year Target — it's a forecast. The target should require
  the company to become a different version of itself.
- **Impossible target.** A target nobody on the leadership team
  privately believes is achievable demoralizes the organization
  rather than motivating it. Push for ambitious-but-credible.
- **Rebranding the 3-Year Picture as a 10-Year Target.** Some teams
  describe the company three years out and call it the long target.
  Different artifacts, different functions — keep them distinct.
- **No cascade alignment.** Setting a 10-Year Target that cannot be
  reached by any plausible 3-Year Picture / 1-Year Plan / Rocks
  cascade. The target has to be reachable through the layers below
  it, not by some unrelated route.

## When to apply

- **Phase 2 V/TO session**, as the answer to question 3 of the eight
  V/TO questions
- **Annual Planning Session**, as part of the Vision page walkthrough
  — confirm the existing target still pulls the team forward and is
  still ten years out (or roll it down into the 3-Year Picture if
  the company has gotten close)
- **Whenever the company has clearly outgrown its current target** —
  set a new one early rather than waiting for the formal review
- **Whenever the leadership team disagrees about long-range
  direction** — usually a signal that the existing target is too
  vague to anchor on
