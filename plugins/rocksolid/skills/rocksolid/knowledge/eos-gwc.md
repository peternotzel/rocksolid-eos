# GWC — Get It, Want It, Capacity to Do It

GWC™ is the three-part filter the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™)
uses to decide whether a specific person is in the right seat. It is the
"right seat" half of the broader Right Person, Right Seat framework — Core
Values handle the "right person" half, and GWC handles seat fit. The three
letters stand for Get it, Want it, and Capacity to do it. The rule that
makes the filter useful is its binary nature: all three have to be yes. Two
out of three is not a partial pass — it is a fail. Tolerance for ambiguity
on this question is the single most common reason that the rest of EOS
quietly stops working, because seats with the wrong people in them break the
Accountability Chart, and a broken Accountability Chart breaks every
downstream tool.

GWC is also one of the easiest tools in the system to apply. It is a 15-to-30
minute conversation per person per seat, repeated whenever a placement
decision is made. There is no spreadsheet, no model, no scoring rubric — just
three questions, each answered yes or no.

## Get it

"Get it" is the intuitive understanding of what the seat is actually for —
its purpose inside the business, the rhythm of the work it requires, how it
connects to the rest of the organization, and why it matters. A person who
gets it can be dropped into a conversation about the seat and immediately
talk about it in a way that sounds right to other people who own similar
seats. They don't have to be told what their job is, they don't have to ask
why they exist, and they don't get blindsided by the recurring patterns of
the role.

The hard part of "Get it" is that it cannot be reliably trained into someone
who doesn't have it. Some people simply "see" a particular kind of role and
some do not. You can sometimes coach a borderline case across the line over
months, but for someone who fundamentally does not get it, no amount of
training, mentoring, or experience converts them into someone who does. The
filter exists to surface that reality early rather than after eighteen
frustrating months.

## Want it

"Want it" is whether the person genuinely wants to do this work, day after
day, on its own terms, at fair compensation. The keyword is "genuinely."
Wanting the title is not wanting it. Wanting the salary is not wanting it.
Tolerating it because the alternative seems worse is not wanting it. Being
talked into it by a manager who needs the seat filled is definitely not
wanting it.

Wickman warns specifically against the management instinct to persuade,
motivate, or over-pay someone into wanting a seat they do not actually want.
That instinct is well-meaning and almost always backfires. People who do not
want a job will eventually disengage, regardless of how much the company is
paying them or how prestigious the title is, and the disengagement will
infect the rest of the team long before the person finally leaves.

## Capacity to do it

"Capacity to do it" is the multi-dimensional question of whether the person
has the bandwidth — across every dimension that bandwidth comes in — to
deliver the seat's five major roles at the level the business needs.
Capacity has many faces:

- **Time.** A role might require 55 working hours a week and the person is
  only available for 40.
- **Mental bandwidth.** The role demands a level of cognitive load,
  context-switching, or strategic thinking the person cannot sustain.
- **Emotional bandwidth.** The role requires resilience under conflict, the
  ability to deliver hard messages, or the stamina to handle constant
  interpersonal friction.
- **Physical capacity.** The role demands stamina, presence, or physical
  attributes the person doesn't have.
- **Skills.** The role requires technical or domain expertise the person has
  not yet developed and cannot develop in the relevant timeframe.
- **Judgment.** The role requires making consequential decisions under
  ambiguity, and the person's judgment isn't yet at that level.

A no on capacity does not mean the person is lesser, weaker, or less
valuable. It means the match between this specific person and this specific
seat is wrong today. The right move is almost always a different seat,
not termination.

## Why it has to be binary

Wickman insists on the binary nature of GWC because the alternative is the
slow death of every accountability process in the company. A "mostly yes" on
Get it becomes a culture of subtle confusion about what each role is for. A
"mostly yes" on Want it becomes the gradual disengagement of a person who
cannot quite name why they're tired all the time. A "mostly yes" on Capacity
becomes constant near-misses that the team papers over until they can't
anymore.

The binary discipline forces honesty earlier. A team that uses GWC strictly
will, over time, surface seat-fit problems within weeks instead of years,
and will resolve them while they are still cheap to resolve.

## How GWC interacts with the People Analyzer

The People Analyzer™ grid combines GWC with Core Values scoring on a single
page. The Core Values columns (left half of the grid) use a three-symbol
fuzzy scale: `+`, `+/-`, `-`. The GWC columns (right half) use a two-symbol
binary scale: `Y` or `N`. There is **no** `+/-` allowed in the GWC columns.
Any tool, template, or form that lets a leader put a "mostly" mark in a GWC
column has broken the discipline at the form level.

The grid also makes the relationship between the two halves visible. A
person can be a "right person, wrong seat" — strong on Core Values, weak on
GWC for their current seat — and the right move is to find them a different
seat, not to let them go. Conversely, a person can be a "wrong person, right
seat" — strong on GWC for their seat, weak on Core Values — and despite the
short-term performance the right move is to let them go before the values
mismatch erodes the culture.

## How to conduct a GWC assessment

A GWC assessment is a focused 15-to-30 minute conversation, usually between
the seat holder's direct manager and the seat holder. It happens at three
points:

- **Before placing someone into a new seat** (hiring, promotion, lateral
  move). Score the candidate against GWC for the destination seat.
- **As part of every quarterly review cycle.** Re-score the person against
  their current seat. Things change — capacity in particular can shift as
  business needs change.
- **Whenever a manager has a nagging "something is off" feeling about a
  team member.** GWC usually surfaces what.

The questions are simple and direct: Does this person genuinely get the
purpose of the seat? Do they actually want to be doing this work? Do they
have the capacity, across every dimension, to deliver the five major roles
at the level the business needs? Each gets answered yes or no.

## Common failure modes

The three most common shapes of seat-fit failure:

### "Wants it" but doesn't "Get it"

The classic enthusiastic-but-confused hire. The person is bought in, working
hard, and wants to succeed, but they don't have the intuitive feel for what
the seat is for. They make decisions that look right to them and wrong to
everyone else who works around the seat. Wrong seat. The fix is rarely
training; it is moving them.

### "Gets it" but "Doesn't want it"

The painful pattern of the person who is clearly capable of doing the job
well — and may even have done it well in the past — but no longer wants to
be doing it. They show up, deliver competent work, and slowly disengage.
Wrong seat. The fix is to find a seat they actually want, or to part ways.
Persuading them to keep doing this job longer makes both sides unhappy.

### "Gets it and wants it" but lacks Capacity

The hardest case because everyone is on the same side. The person
understands the seat, genuinely wants it, and is trying — but they cannot
deliver the five major roles at the level the business needs. Wrong seat,
still. Capacity gaps are real and they don't go away through effort alone.
The compassionate move is to acknowledge the gap, find a seat where their
capacity matches the demands, and stop pretending the current placement
will work out.

## Criteria / Rules

- The filter has exactly three questions: Get it, Want it, Capacity to do
  it. Always in that order, never collapsed.
- Each question is answered binary: Y or N. No "mostly," no "kind of," no
  `+/-`.
- All three must be Y for the person to be in the right seat. One N is a
  fail.
- A capacity failure is multi-dimensional — the assessment covers time,
  mental bandwidth, emotional bandwidth, physical capacity, skills, and
  judgment.
- GWC is evaluated against a specific seat. The same person can be a Y/Y/Y
  on one seat and N on another.

## Common pitfalls

- Tolerating a "mostly yes" because the alternative conversation is hard.
- Promising training to fix a "doesn't get it" — almost never works.
- Persuading or over-paying someone into "wanting it" — backfires within
  months.
- Treating capacity failures as character flaws instead of seat-fit
  mismatches.
- Skipping GWC because the leadership team is uncomfortable applying the
  filter to themselves.
- Letting a single Core Values "+" override a GWC "N" — the two filters
  are independent, and a wrong seat is a wrong seat regardless of culture
  fit.

## When to apply

- Phase 1: as soon as the Accountability Chart structure exists and names
  start being placed
- Every hiring decision before an offer goes out
- Every quarterly review cycle for every person
- Whenever a leader has a recurring uneasy feeling about a team member's
  fit
- Before promoting anyone — score them against the new seat
