# The Scorecard

The Scorecard™ is the fourth tool installed by the Entrepreneurial Operating
System™ (EOS™), and it closes the data feedback loop around the weekly
leadership rhythm. Most leadership teams steer their businesses by reading
financial statements that arrive two to six weeks after the period they
describe. By the time the numbers land, the past is unchangeable. The
Scorecard exists to fix that lag: it surfaces a small set of weekly numbers
that move *before* the financial outcomes move, so that the team can react
while there is still time to change what happens next. It is not a richer
P&L, not a dashboard for executives to admire, and not a place to dump every
metric the business already collects. It is a deliberately tiny, deliberately
weekly, deliberately leading-indicator instrument that the leadership team
walks through every Monday morning and uses to decide what to talk about next.

## Why a Scorecard exists

A traditional P&L tells the team what already happened — revenue booked,
expenses incurred, profit recognized. That information is necessary but
fundamentally backward-looking. By the time a missed quarter shows up on the
income statement, the activities that would have produced a different
outcome are weeks behind the team. The Scorecard inverts that timeline by
tracking the weekly activities whose results predict the eventual P&L.
Because each activity-based number changes earlier in the chain than the
financial outcome, reacting to a Scorecard miss in week two of a quarter can
prevent the financial miss in week twelve.

The other reason for the Scorecard is psychological: weekly numbers, scored
against weekly goals, generate weekly conversations. Without them, the
leadership team's weekly meeting rapidly degenerates into status updates,
storytelling, and circular discussion. The Scorecard gives the meeting a
factual spine to anchor on.

## The 5-to-15 rule (with the bias toward 5)

A Scorecard carries between five and fifteen weekly numbers. The defensible
upper limit is fifteen; the preferred target is closer to five. The reasoning
is the same focus principle that drives the Rocks rule: when everything is
measured, nothing is. A team trying to track twenty metrics every week will
end up actually paying attention to none of them. A team tracking five will
internalize each one, develop a felt sense of what the normal range looks
like, and notice the moment something is drifting.

Most first-draft Scorecards land somewhere between eight and twelve numbers,
and most evolve downward over the first few months as the team discovers
which numbers are decisive and which are merely informative.

## Activity-based, not P&L-based

Every Scorecard number must be a leading indicator — an activity that
happens earlier in the chain than the financial outcome it predicts. This is
the rule that separates a Scorecard from any other dashboard the team may
already maintain.

The categorical difference matters. Examples of typical leading-indicator
shapes (described rather than copied from the source):

- A weekly count of inbound qualified leads
- A weekly count of activities at each named stage of the sales process
- A weekly cash-position snapshot at a fixed weekday
- A weekly accounts-receivable-aging marker
- A weekly customer satisfaction signal captured proactively
- A weekly delivery or production throughput figure
- A weekly utilization or capacity figure for the operations function

The unifying property is that each of these numbers moves earlier in the
chain than the trailing P&L outcome, so reacting to the number actually
changes the outcome. A monthly revenue figure does not pass this test — it
is the outcome itself, observed after the fact.

## One named owner per number

Each row on the Scorecard has exactly one named owner from the leadership
team. Not a department, not a committee, not "whoever runs the spreadsheet."
A single human being whose reputation is on the line for that number every
week.

The temptation, especially with finance-heavy numbers, is to assign the
owner to the person who keys the data into the file. That is a clerical
role, not an ownership role. The owner is the leader of the function that
*drives* the number — the head of sales for the funnel numbers, the head of
operations for the throughput numbers, the head of finance for the cash and
receivables numbers. The data-entry assignment is a separate concern.

This single-owner discipline mirrors the Rocks™ rule and the broader EOS
pattern: ambiguous ownership produces no ownership at all. A number with two
owners is a number nobody is accountable for.

## Goals tied to the 1-Year Plan

Each number on the Scorecard has an explicit weekly goal — the threshold
that distinguishes "on track" from "off track" for that week. The weekly
goal is not a stretch aspiration; it is the actual line below which the team
agrees the number is in trouble.

In a fully-implemented EOS environment (Phase 2 onward, after the V/TO™
exists), each weekly goal derives from the 1-Year Plan section of the
Vision/Traction Organizer™. That linkage ensures the weekly data loop is
mathematically tied to the annual vision: hitting every weekly goal should,
by construction, deliver the annual plan.

In Phase 1, however — when the skill may be installing a Scorecard before
the V/TO has been built — the goals come from a pragmatic team agreement
about what good looks like for the next quarter or two. These pragmatic
targets get realigned to the 1-Year Plan as soon as the V/TO exists. This
realignment is one of the forward-coherence checks the skill should run when
it crosses the Phase 1 / Phase 2 boundary.

## The desert-island framing

The conceptual exercise the leadership team uses to identify the right
numbers (paraphrased from the book): imagine the leadership team is suddenly
cut off from the business — no email, no phone, no in-person visits — and
must run the company from a single sheet of paper containing only a handful
of weekly numbers. Which numbers must be on that sheet for the team to know
whether the business is healthy and to make the next decision correctly?

The forcing function of the exercise is that almost every metric the team
currently tracks fails the test. Most numbers are interesting but not
decisive, and the exercise quickly strips them away. What remains is the
short list of numbers that are genuinely load-bearing, which is why the
final Scorecard is closer to five than fifteen.

## The 13-week rolling grid format

Structurally, the Scorecard is a wide grid. The columns from left to right
are:

1. **WHO** — the named owner of each number (leftmost)
2. **MEASURABLE** — the metric description
3. **GOAL** — the weekly target
4. **Thirteen dated week-ending columns** showing the weekly actuals

As each new week is entered on the right edge, the oldest week falls off the
visible grid (but is preserved in historical storage). The visible window is
always exactly one quarter of weeks, which is enough context to distinguish
noise from signal: a single off-week is just noise, but three off-weeks in a
row is a real trend that the team should be discussing.

The week columns are explicitly dated (week-ending dates, not generic "Week
1, Week 2" labels). This matters because dated columns ground the data in
real calendar time and make it impossible to lose track of which week is
which when the L10™ scrolls past it on a Monday morning.

The format is weekly, not monthly. A monthly Scorecard is a different
artifact serving a different purpose.

## The WHO column is leftmost — and that is structural

A load-bearing visual convention from the source: the WHO (owner) column is
the leftmost column on the Scorecard, before the metric name and before the
goal value. This is not a layout preference — it is a deliberate elevation
of ownership. The first thing the eye sees on every row is the name of the
person who is on the hook for that number.

A Scorecard that hides ownership in a footer, in a separate tab, or as the
last column on the right has structurally demoted ownership and predictably
ends up with numbers nobody actually owns. Templates the skill produces
should always preserve WHO as the leftmost column.

## Red-flagging — turning the grid into a trigger

When a weekly actual misses its goal, the cell is visually marked, typically
shaded red. The mark exists for one purpose: to pull the eye during the L10
Scorecard Review segment so that the off-track number is impossible to miss
in the five seconds the team spends on each row. Red cells drop into the
Issues List for handling later in the meeting; unflagged cells flow past in
silence.

This visual trigger is what distinguishes an active Scorecard from a passive
log. Without the red-flag mechanism, the Scorecard becomes a spreadsheet
that nobody opens because there is nothing on it to react to.

## Three rules of thumb

The book consolidates the Scorecard discipline into three short rules:

1. **Activity-based, not P&L-based.** Every number must be a leading
   indicator that moves before the financial outcome moves. Trailing
   numbers belong on the monthly P&L review, not on the Scorecard.
2. **Complement, not replacement.** The Scorecard sits *alongside* the
   monthly and quarterly financial review, not in place of it. Both layers
   are needed. The Scorecard provides the early signal; the P&L provides
   the eventual confirmation.
3. **Red-flag for visibility.** Visual marking of misses creates the focus
   that makes the weekly review productive. Without the marks, the eye
   skims past the data and the meeting accomplishes nothing.

## Evolution — the first draft is about 85% right

Wickman is explicit that the first version of a Scorecard is not the final
version. Most leadership teams take between one and three months of weekly
use before they settle on a stable list. Over that window the wrong numbers
get pruned (usually because the team realizes they are vanity, lagging, or
unactionable), the right numbers get sharper definitions, and goals get
recalibrated against the actual variability of the data.

The implication for the skill is to discourage perfectionism in the
Scorecard-design session. A team that tries to design the perfect Scorecard
on day one will spend hours debating numbers that turn out to be wrong
anyway. A team that writes down a credible first draft and starts using it
on Monday will iterate to the right answer within a quarter.

## Connection to the Level 10 Meeting

The Scorecard does not exist in isolation — it is the second item on the
weekly L10 agenda, immediately after the Segue. The Scorecard Review segment
is fixed at five minutes, which is enough time for each leader to walk
through their numbers, mark each one as on-track or off-track, and drop any
red-flagged number to the Issues List for IDS™ later in the meeting.

The deliberate compression matters. The Scorecard Review is **report-only**:
no discussion happens here, no problem-solving, no debate about why a number
is off. The instinct to launch into a fix the moment a bad number appears is
strong and must be suppressed; otherwise the L10 blows past ninety minutes
and still solves nothing. The off-track number drops to IDS and gets handled
properly later in the meeting.

Without the L10 as the consuming forum, a Scorecard is just a spreadsheet
nobody opens. This is part of why Scorecard is implemented as Tool 4, after
the Meeting Pulse™: the Scorecard needs the meeting to give it weekly
purpose.

## Substance criteria

A Scorecard passes the substance check when:

- It carries between 5 and 15 weekly numbers (not 2–4, not 20+)
- Each number is activity-based — a leading indicator, not a trailing P&L
  figure
- Each number has exactly one named owner from the Accountability Chart™
- Each number has an explicit weekly goal, not just a recorded actual
- At least two weeks of actual data have been entered, so trend visibility
  has begun
- Goals are tied to the 1-Year Plan in Phase 2+, or to a pragmatic team
  target in Phase 1 (with a realignment scheduled)
- A visible red-flag mechanism is in place for misses (shading, marker, or
  equivalent)
- The Scorecard is actively used in the L10 Scorecard Review segment, with
  L10 notes referencing it
- The format is a rolling 13-week grid with explicitly dated week-ending
  columns (not monthly summaries)
- The leftmost column is WHO — ownership elevated, not buried

## Common pitfalls

- **P&L numbers in disguise.** Putting monthly revenue or monthly profit on
  the Scorecard turns it into a slow dashboard that cannot drive weekly
  action. Replace with the leading activities that produce those numbers.
- **Twenty rows instead of seven.** Overloads the team, kills the focus
  benefit, and predicts that nobody will internalize any number. Cut.
- **Owner = the data-entry person.** Defaults to no owner. The owner is
  the leader of the function that *drives* the number, not the person who
  fills in the cell.
- **No goal column.** A number without a goal cannot be on-track or
  off-track, so the report-only convention collapses and the segment
  turns into open discussion.
- **No red-flag mechanism.** The team's eye glides past the off-track
  numbers and the Scorecard never triggers any IDS work.
- **Trying to perfect the first draft.** Burns hours on a list that will
  evolve in production anyway. Ship a credible first draft and iterate
  weekly.
- **Treating the Scorecard segment as a discussion.** The Scorecard Review
  is report-only. Discussion of misses happens in IDS, not here.
- **Hiding the WHO column.** Demotes ownership and predicts orphaned
  numbers. Keep WHO leftmost.
- **Monthly columns instead of weekly.** Loses the early-warning property
  that is the entire reason the tool exists.

## When to apply

- **Phase 1**, after the Accountability Chart, Rocks, and Meeting Pulse are
  in place — the Scorecard is the fourth tool installed
- **Every week**, as item #2 of the L10 agenda, in a five-minute report-only
  walk
- **Quarterly Planning Session**, as part of the previous-quarter review and
  to recalibrate goals if the underlying business has shifted
- **Phase 2 transition**, when the V/TO is built — realign Scorecard goals
  to the 1-Year Plan
- **First 1-3 months of use**, expect actively pruning and reshaping the
  number list as the team discovers what is decisive
