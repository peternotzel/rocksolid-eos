# The Meeting Pulse

The Meeting Pulse™ is the cardiac rhythm of the Entrepreneurial Operating
System™ (EOS™). It is not a single meeting type — it is a system of three
meeting types operating at three different tempos, each with its own
purpose, length, agenda, and discipline. The three tempos are not
substitutes for each other; they are layered, and removing any one of the
three causes the other two to deform. Wickman places the Meeting Pulse third
in the implementation order, immediately after Rocks™, because once
priorities are set, the team needs a cadence that keeps those priorities
visible and forces the resolution of issues that threaten them. Without
that cadence, Rocks slide, issues accumulate, and the ninety-day rhythm
collapses inside a couple of quarters.

## The three tempos

The Meeting Pulse has exactly three meeting types running at three
different frequencies:

| Meeting | Tempo | Length | Audience | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly Level 10™ Meeting (L10™) | Every week, same day, same time | 90 minutes | Leadership team only | On-site is fine |
| Quarterly Planning Session | Every 90 days | Full day (~8 hours) | Leadership team only | Off-site |
| Annual Planning Session | Once per year | Two full days | Leadership team only | Off-site, ideally requires travel |

Together these three form the heartbeat of the organization. The weekly
beat catches drift before it accumulates. The quarterly beat resets
priorities and clears the issue backlog. The annual beat refreshes the
strategic picture itself.

## The Weekly L10 — drift correction

The L10 is the seven-day cycle. It exists to keep the leadership team
focused on the small handful of things that matter week to week: the
Scorecard™ numbers, the Rocks, customer and employee signals, the To-Do
list, and the Issues List. It is the weekly moment of truth for
accountability — every owner reports on their numbers and their Rocks in
front of their peers, and any miss drops to the Issues Solving Track
(IDS™) inside the same meeting.

The defining ground rules of the L10:

- Same day of the week, same start time, every week.
- Ninety minutes, with a hard stop.
- Same printed agenda every time.
- Held even when people are absent — the meeting is never skipped or
  rescheduled.
- The agenda allocates sixty of the ninety minutes to IDS, because that
  is where the leverage is.

The weekly cadence is the fastest of the three and exists to prevent
drift between quarterly resets. A team running a healthy L10 walks into
their quarterly with maybe a dozen unresolved strategic issues. A team
without an L10 walks into their quarterly with a hundred — and burns the
whole day triaging instead of planning.

## The Quarterly Planning Session — priority reset

The Quarterly Planning Session is the ninety-day cycle. It is the forum
where the leadership team scores the previous quarter, refreshes the
V/TO™, sets the next quarter's Rocks, and tackles the biggest issues that
have accumulated. It runs as a full day, not a half day, off-site
whenever possible, with the leadership team only.

Standard agenda walked in order:

1. **Segue.** Each person shares best news (business and personal),
   what's working and what isn't, and their expectations for the day.
2. **Review previous quarter.** Last quarter's Scorecard numbers and
   Rocks scored binary. Target is eighty percent or better Rock
   completion.
3. **Review the V/TO.** Refresh memory, resolve loss of alignment,
   update the V/TO Issues List section.
4. **Establish next quarter's Rocks.** Run the eight-step Rock-setting
   process in miniature using the Issues List as raw material.
5. **Tackle key issues.** Whatever time remains (typically one to four
   hours) goes into IDS on the biggest issues, in priority order.
6. **Next steps.** Who does what after the meeting, what gets
   communicated to the organization.
7. **Conclude.** Feedback round, expectations-met check, 1-to-10 rating.

Pre-work: every attendee arrives with their own list of issues and
candidate priorities for the coming quarter, and the V/TO is current as
of the day before.

## The Annual Planning Session — strategic refresh

The Annual Planning Session is the 365-day cycle. It is two full days,
off-site, ideally somewhere that requires travel so returning to the
office is not a tempting option. Like the others it is leadership team
only, and it is scheduled at least a quarter in advance — usually
significantly more.

The two-day shape:

**Day One** focuses on backward review and strategic refresh. The team
walks accomplishments and lessons from the previous year, scores the
prior year's goals and the prior quarter's Rocks, runs an open-room
team-health exercise where each leader receives spoken feedback and
commits to one specific change for the coming year, runs a SWOT to
populate an Issues List for Day Two, and then walks the V/TO from Core
Values down through the 1-Year Plan — challenging Core Focus and the
10-Year Target, throwing out last year's 3-Year Picture entirely and
building a new one, and setting next year's numbers and three-to-seven
goals.

**Day Two** focuses on forward execution. It mirrors the quarterly
agenda — set Q1 Rocks, tackle key issues via IDS, agree next steps,
conclude. The annual is structured this way so that it also serves as
that quarter's quarterly session, which means the team gets the strategic
refresh and the priority reset in a single off-site rather than holding
two separate sessions a few weeks apart.

The team eats dinner together at the end of Day One — that meal is part
of the team-health investment, not optional decoration.

## How the three tempos interlock

Each tempo feeds the one above it and depends on the one below it. The
weekly L10 captures issues mid-week and works them inside its sixty
minutes of IDS, leaving only strategic-altitude items unresolved by the
end of each quarter. The quarterly session takes those leftover items
plus the team's accumulated thinking and resets the priorities for the
next ninety days. The annual session takes the four quarters' worth of
unresolved strategic questions and refreshes the entire vision and
1-Year Plan, then sets Q1 Rocks to feed the cycle below it.

The cascade also runs downward. The annual sets the year's goals and
the 1-Year Plan. The quarterly draws Rocks from that 1-Year Plan, in
priority order. The weekly walks those Rocks every Monday and produces
To-Dos that drive day-to-day action. From annual vision to weekly action
is a clean four-step cascade, and each step has its own forum.

## Why each cadence matters in its own right

- **Weekly = drift correction.** Without it, small issues compound into
  large ones and quarterly sessions become firefights.
- **Quarterly = priority reset.** Without it, the team has no forum for
  stepping back and the L10 turns into pure operations.
- **Annual = strategic refresh.** Without it, quarterly priorities slowly
  drift away from any coherent direction.

A healthy implementation has all three running simultaneously. A common
failure pattern is to install the L10 enthusiastically, declare victory,
and never get around to the quarterly. Six months later the team is
running disciplined weekly meetings on stale priorities.

## The buildup effect at quarterly and annual scales

The same buildup effect that makes the 90-Day World work amplifies at the
annual. A two-day off-site that has been on the calendar since January
generates a year's worth of accumulated thinking that gets discharged in
those forty hours. A two-day session scheduled two weeks in advance
produces nothing of the kind. Pre-scheduling is what makes the entire
Pulse work.

## The same-day-same-time discipline at the weekly level

At the weekly level, the discipline is not just that the L10 happens but
that it happens on the same day at the same time, every week. That
consistency is what removes the meeting from the team's mental load —
they stop deciding whether to attend, stop juggling it against other
meetings, stop negotiating its time with each other. It becomes a fact
of the week, not a decision. The moment the team starts moving the L10
to accommodate other things, it begins decaying.

## Criteria / Rules

**Hard rules (must pass for the Pulse to count as installed):**

- A Weekly L10 is on the calendar, same day, same time, every week.
- A Quarterly Planning Session is scheduled for each of the next four
  quarters, full-day, off-site.
- An Annual Planning Session is scheduled for the next year, two days,
  off-site.
- The leadership team is the only attendee group for all three meeting
  types.
- The ninety-minute weekly hard stop is enforced.
- The full-day length of the quarterly is enforced.
- The two-day length of the annual is enforced.

**Soft rules (context-dependent):**

- Off-site is preferred for quarterly and annual, but Phase 1 teams may
  start on-site if travel is impossible.
- Pre-scheduling at least a year in advance is preferred over
  quarter-to-quarter scheduling.

## Common pitfalls

- **Installing the L10 only.** The single most common implementation
  failure. The L10 alone produces drift, not progress.
- **Compressing the quarterly to a half day.** Half days do not produce
  the buildup discharge or the time for IDS.
- **Holding sessions in the office.** The office is full of
  interruptions; off-site is part of the mechanism, not a perk.
- **Inviting the whole company to the quarterly.** Quarterly and annual
  are leadership-team-only by design.
- **Letting the L10 slide to a different day "just this week."** That
  is the first step in the meeting decaying out of existence.
- **Skipping a quarterly when "things are too busy."** This is exactly
  when the quarterly is most needed, not least.

## When to apply

- Phase 1 onboarding — establish all three calendars before any other
  Traction tool is installed.
- Every quarterly review — confirm next quarter's date is still locked.
- Every annual review — set the four quarterlies plus next annual
  before the team leaves the room.
