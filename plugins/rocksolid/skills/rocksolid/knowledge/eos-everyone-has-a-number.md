# Everyone Has a Number

"Everyone Has a Number" is Tool 7 of the Entrepreneurial Operating System™
(EOS™) and the final tool installed. It extends the Scorecard™ discipline
that has been running at the leadership level all the way down to every
person in the organization. The premise is simple: in a healthy
business, every employee — not just the leadership team — owns at least
one weekly, activity-based number they can answer for. The discipline
sounds mechanical on the surface but is really about accountability.
Numbers cut through subjective conversation between managers and reports,
create unambiguous expectations, surface problems early (before they
show up as trailing financial pain), and give the right people the
clarity they want while exposing the wrong people in the wrong seats.
When it is rolled out properly, the whole organization operates on the
same kind of measurement discipline the leadership team has been
practicing since Tool 4.

## Why this is the last tool

Everyone Has a Number sits at the end of the implementation sequence
because it depends on nearly every earlier tool already being in place:

- **The Accountability Chart™** has to be solid so that each person's
  role and expected contributions are unambiguous. Rolling numbers out
  on an unclear or unstable chart produces numbers that measure the
  wrong things or cannot be assigned to a single owner.
- **The Scorecard has to be running** at the leadership level so that
  the organization already has a working model of weekly,
  activity-based measurement. Leadership has to have lived the
  discipline before it can credibly extend it.
- **Core Processes have to be documented** under Tool 6 so that
  individual numbers have a meaningful source to attach to. A number
  detached from any documented process measures activity in a vacuum
  and drifts over time.
- **Operating discipline has to exist** — a running weekly L10™
  cadence, strong management habits, the LMA™ practice of Lead /
  Manage / Accountability — so that numbers actually get tracked,
  discussed, and acted on once they are assigned.

Without those foundations, pushing numbers out to everyone produces
resentment and spreadsheet theater rather than real accountability.
People without a stable seat, without a running weekly cadence, and
without documented processes will experience new numbers as surveillance
rather than clarity. The sequencing is what makes the tool work.

## The concept — downstream accountability

Every person in the organization, including non-leadership team members,
has one to three weekly, activity-based numbers they own. These are
leading-indicator measurements, not lagging financial outcomes. Each
person can answer, in any given week, "what's my number, and did I hit
it?"

The specific number varies enormously by role — an operations employee
might own an accuracy, turnaround, or throughput measurement; a
customer-facing role might own a response-time, contact-count, or
retention measurement; a production role might own an output or quality
measurement. What matters is not the topic of the number but its
properties: it is weekly, it is measurable, it is genuinely controlled
by the person who owns it, and it is traceable upward into the larger
measurement system.

The benefits Wickman cites for putting numbers on people at every level:

- **Numbers cut through subjective conversation.** Manager-report
  discussions about performance stop being a debate about attitude
  and start being a conversation about evidence.
- **Expectations become unambiguous.** The person with the number
  knows exactly what is expected each week; there is no gap between
  their understanding and the manager's.
- **The right people appreciate the clarity.** High performers in
  the right seats tend to welcome a number they can point at. The
  people who resist hard are usually signaling they are in the wrong
  seat, which is information the organization needs.
- **Problems surface early.** Activity-based leading indicators
  catch issues weeks or months before they show up in lagging
  financial results, which is how fixes can happen while they are
  still cheap.

## The properties of a valid number

Not every metric qualifies as an "Everyone Has a Number" number. A valid
number has specific properties:

- **Weekly frequency.** Not monthly, not quarterly, not project-based
  milestones. The measurement cycle is one week. This is what makes
  the number actionable — a person can see it, hit or miss, and
  adjust within days rather than after a whole quarter has gone by.
- **Activity-based and leading.** The number measures something the
  person does, not something that happens to them later as a result
  of many people's actions. Revenue is a lagging indicator; calls
  made, contracts drafted, tickets resolved, units produced are
  leading indicators.
- **Owned by exactly one person.** Shared ownership is non-ownership.
  The number has one name on it, and that name is accountable for
  hitting it.
- **Has a goal.** A number without a target is just information, not
  accountability. The goal is the line the person is trying to cross
  each week.
- **Traceable to a Core Process.** The number measures a step or an
  outcome of one of the documented core processes from Tool 6. A
  number with no process source is measuring activity in a vacuum,
  and usually degrades into a proxy for "looking busy" rather than
  "producing results."
- **Rolls up through the Accountability Chart.** The number feeds the
  manager's number, which feeds a leadership team member's Scorecard
  number, which feeds the leadership team's view of the business.
  The whole chain is visible.

## The cascade — from the Scorecard to the floor

Everyone Has a Number creates a measurement cascade that runs from the
leadership Scorecard all the way down to individual daily activity. The
cascade works in both directions:

```
Leadership Scorecard (Tool 4)
         |
         | rolls up from
         v
Department / seat-level numbers
         |
         | rolls up from
         v
Individual weekly numbers (Tool 7)
         |
         | reflects
         v
Daily activity on documented Core Processes (Tool 6)
```

Looking top-down, every Scorecard number should eventually decompose
into a set of individual numbers that together produce it. Looking
bottom-up, every individual number should eventually trace into a
leadership Scorecard number — if a personal number does not roll up to
anything the leadership team is watching, either the number is
measuring the wrong thing or the Scorecard is missing a column.

Where do the individual numbers come from in practice? The
Accountability Chart is the best starting point. Each seat on the
chart has a handful of major roles. For each seat, the drafting
process is: look at the roles, identify which ones can be measured
weekly, and turn one to three of them into numbers. The seat's
numbers then roll up into the seat's manager's numbers and so on.

## Rollout strategy

The book is realistic about the rollout: don't try to push numbers onto
everyone in week one. The right sequence is to cascade out from the
leadership team rather than to announce a company-wide measurement
program.

- **Start with the leadership team's direct reports.** The first tier
  below the leadership team — the managers and individual
  contributors who report directly to leadership — is where the
  rollout begins. Leadership already has numbers (the Scorecard), and
  the direct reports are the natural next ring.
- **Stabilize that tier before going further.** Give the first tier
  enough weeks to internalize the discipline, surface issues in
  their own weekly management conversations, and adjust their
  numbers as they learn what works.
- **Cascade outward tier by tier.** Once the first tier is stable,
  the rollout extends to the next layer, then the next, using the
  same process. Each tier becomes responsible for defining and
  tracking the numbers of the tier below it.
- **Expect months, not weeks.** Rolling numbers out to everyone
  typically begins several months after the Scorecard has been
  stable, and a full-company rollout can take a year or more
  depending on company size and number of layers.

Attempting to push numbers onto the whole company in a single
announcement almost always produces resentment, pushback, and
low-quality numbers picked under time pressure. The cascade approach
protects the quality of the discipline by keeping each tier
responsible for the tier below it.

## Substance criteria

An Everyone Has a Number effort passes the substance check when:

- **Every person** in the organization (not just leadership team
  members) has at least one weekly activity-based number
- Each number has a named owner (exactly one person), a target, a
  weekly frequency, and a clear data source
- Each number is **activity-based and leading**, not a lagging
  financial outcome
- Each number is **anchored in a documented core process** from
  Tool 6
- Each number **traces upward through the Accountability Chart** to
  a leadership Scorecard number — the chain is visible end to end
- A **centralized directory** (for example, a measurables directory
  file or equivalent) lists every person alongside their number(s)
  and targets
- The discipline was rolled out by **cascading from the leadership
  team's direct reports outward**, not by a company-wide blast
- Every number is reviewed on a weekly cadence in the appropriate
  management conversation, not left to accumulate

## Common pitfalls

- **Numbers that aren't activity-based.** Measuring a lagging
  outcome (revenue, profit, customer satisfaction score) at the
  individual level. The person cannot directly control the number
  week to week, which converts the measurement into frustration.
- **Numbers without a Core Process source.** Making up a number
  that isn't tied to any documented workflow. The number drifts
  into "looking busy" activity rather than process-driven work.
- **Numbers that don't roll up.** Individual numbers that have no
  clear line to a leadership Scorecard number. The chain of
  accountability is broken and the number becomes an island.
- **Shared ownership.** Two or three people "sharing" a number. No
  one is accountable, everyone assumes someone else is watching,
  and the discipline collapses.
- **Missing the target.** A number with no goal is just a
  measurement, not accountability. The person has nothing to hit
  or miss.
- **Rolling out before the foundations exist.** Announcing numbers
  before the Accountability Chart is stable, the Scorecard is
  running, the Core Processes are documented, or the L10 cadence
  is in place. Produces resentment and low-quality numbers.
- **Big-bang rollout.** Pushing numbers to the whole company in
  week one instead of cascading tier by tier from leadership
  outward. The quality of the numbers is low because they were
  picked under time pressure.
- **Too many numbers per person.** Assigning five or more numbers
  to a single role. Focus is lost and nothing gets hit reliably.
  One to three is the right range.
- **Unreviewed numbers.** Assigning numbers and then never
  actually looking at them in a weekly management conversation.
  The numbers degrade into performative spreadsheets.

## When to apply

- **Phase 3 kickoff**, as the seventh and final foundational tool,
  after People, Rocks, Meeting Pulse™, Scorecard, V/TO™, and Core
  Processes are all in place
- **During Annual Planning**, as part of the Vision page
  walkthrough — confirm that the measurement cascade still lines
  up with the updated 1-Year Plan and Scorecard
- **Whenever the Accountability Chart changes** — a restructured
  seat means the numbers attached to that seat need to be
  reviewed and reassigned
- **Whenever a Core Process is rewritten** — any individual
  numbers anchored in that process need to be re-examined for
  continued validity
- **Whenever a new hire joins** — part of onboarding is defining
  the new person's weekly number before they complete their
  first full month
