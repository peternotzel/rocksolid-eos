# The Issues Solving Track (IDS)

The Issues Solving Track™ — known by its three-letter shorthand IDS™ — is
the discipline that runs inside the sixty-minute issue-resolution segment of
every Level 10™ Meeting (L10™), and it is the most important habit a
leadership team using the Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™) can
develop. The discipline addresses the most common pathology of leadership
meetings: long, swirling conversations that neither identify what the real
problem is nor produce a clear next action. IDS replaces that pattern with
three sequential steps — Identify the root issue, Discuss it with each
person speaking once, and Solve it into a concrete next step. The acronym
becomes a verb inside EOS organizations: when a team says "let's IDS this,"
they are committing to the three-step structure rather than another
free-form conversation.

## Why IDS exists

Wickman's argument for installing IDS is empirical. Across hundreds of
leadership teams, the most expensive failure mode is the meeting that
spent forty minutes on an issue, generated a lot of strong opinions,
produced no clear action, and left every attendee with a different
interpretation of what was decided. The next week the same issue
resurfaces because nothing actually moved. IDS is a forcing structure
that prevents that pattern from happening. The three steps are simple to
remember, hard to execute well, and cumulatively responsible for most of
the leverage the L10 produces.

## Identify — find the root issue

The first and longest step is Identify, and the central insight is that
the stated problem is almost never the real problem. What surfaces on the
Issues List is usually a symptom — and chasing the symptom produces a
fix that holds for a week before the same symptom returns wearing a
different shirt.

A few illustrative patterns (paraphrased, not from real cases):

- What looks like a recurring customer complaint about turnaround time
  may actually be a wrong-seat issue at the GWC™ level — the seat
  responsible for delivery is held by someone who does not Get it.
- What looks like a sales pipeline problem may actually be a marketing
  message problem two steps upstream.
- What looks like a personality clash between two leaders may actually
  be an ambiguous accountability boundary on the Accountability Chart™
  that puts them in each other's way structurally.

The team's job in Identify is to peel back the layers until the root
cause is named out loud. This step often consumes more time than Discuss
and Solve combined, and that is expected — once the root is correctly
named, the remaining steps frequently collapse into a few minutes.

A useful framing that helps the Identify step run faster: before the
team begins peeling, the person raising the issue states which of three
types it is, because the resolution path differs depending on the type.

1. **A genuine problem that needs solving.** The team will work it
   through to an action.
2. **A piece of information that needs to be communicated and
   acknowledged.** No action is needed; the team just needs to confirm
   shared understanding.
3. **An idea or opportunity that needs feedback or a green light.** The
   owner is asking for a go/no-go, not a solution.

Naming the type up front prevents the team from chasing a solution when
what is actually needed is alignment, or chasing alignment when what is
actually needed is a yes/no decision.

## Discuss — every voice once

Once the real issue has been named, the team moves into Discuss. The
governing rule is that every participant gets one full turn to say what
they think — and one turn only. Repeating a position a second time is
not discussion; it is politicking. When the same points start cycling
around the room, the issue is ready to move into Solve.

Wickman's principle for the substance of the discussion is that
participants are obligated to "fight for the greater good" — to argue
for what is right for the whole business, not for what protects their
own department. A leader who argues against a decision because it would
cost their function some headcount, even though it is clearly the right
call for the company, is failing the standard. A healthy leadership team
holds each other to this norm out loud.

What does *not* happen in Discuss: side conversations, interrupting the
person whose turn it is, looping back to repeat a point already made,
debating the framing of the issue (that work belonged in Identify), or
proposing solutions before everyone has been heard.

### Tangent handling

A common failure inside Discuss is the tangent — someone raises an
adjacent topic that pulls the team away from the issue actually under
discussion. Anyone in the room can call out the drift. When that
happens, the drifting thread is evaluated quickly: if it is itself a
real issue worth working, it gets written onto the Issues List and
picked up later in priority order; if it is not, it is dropped and the
team returns to the current issue. The mechanism matters because
leadership teams routinely chain five or more tangents deep before
realizing they have stopped discussing the original topic, and by that
point the original topic is forgotten.

## Solve — produce action

The output of IDS is almost always action. A solved issue produces one
or more To-Dos with named owners that go onto next week's L10 To-Do
List for accountability. To-Dos are by definition seven-day items —
short-horizon, narrow, and binary. Issues do not stay open across
multiple weeks; they produce action and close.

Wickman identifies three shapes that a Solve can take:

- **Action required.** Someone owns a concrete next step and will
  report done or not-done at the next L10. This is the most common
  shape.
- **Alignment or awareness only.** No action, but the team confirms a
  shared understanding out loud so no one can later claim surprise.
  This is the right shape for issues of type 2 (information).
- **More research needed.** An owner is assigned to gather facts and
  bring them back to a future meeting as a fresh issue. This is the
  right shape when the team genuinely cannot decide yet.

When an issue is solved, someone states the resolution out loud in one
sentence. The team either agrees (the issue is closed) or someone says
"that's not what I heard," in which case discussion reopens. Reopening
is a feature, not a failure — it catches hidden disagreement before it
becomes next week's issue.

## Top-three-first ordering

When the team starts the IDS segment, they do not begin at the top of
the Issues List and work down the page. Instead they pick the top three
items by importance — which is often not the same as the top three on
the page — and work them strictly one at a time in priority order.

There are two reasons for this rule. First, the most important issue is
sometimes near the bottom of the list, because issues land on the list
in the order they are raised, not in order of severity. Starting at the
top means the important issue may never get reached. Second, solving the
most important issue often dissolves several lower-priority issues,
because they were symptoms of the same root cause that just got
addressed. Working in priority order is therefore more efficient as
well as more strategic.

Some weeks the team gets through only one issue in the sixty minutes.
Other weeks they handle ten. Either is fine, provided the work happened
in priority order.

## How IDS connects to the rest of the L10

The Issues List that feeds IDS is not generated at the start of the
meeting. It is a continuous queue maintained between meetings — anyone
on the leadership team can add to it at any time during the week. Bad
Scorecard™ numbers, off-track Rocks™, customer or employee escalations,
and tangents flagged during prior IDS segments all land on it.

The four agenda items that come *before* IDS in the L10 (Scorecard
review, Rock review, customer/employee headlines, To-Do review) are
each a feeder for the Issues List — anything that goes wrong in those
segments drops onto the list rather than getting discussed in place,
and then gets worked in IDS later in the same meeting.

The agenda item that comes *after* IDS (Conclude) reads back the
To-Dos that IDS produced, so every owner has heard their commitment
stated out loud before they leave the room.

## Three coexisting Issues Lists

Not every issue belongs at the L10. Wickman distinguishes three Issues
Lists that coexist in a healthy organization:

1. **The V/TO™ Issues List** — long-horizon items that do not need
   solving this week or this quarter, but must not be forgotten. These
   get worked at the Quarterly Planning Session, not the L10.
2. **The weekly leadership Issues List** — strategic issues at the
   leadership level. Off-track company Rocks, bad key numbers, major
   client or employee escalations, systemic process problems. These
   get worked at the L10.
3. **The departmental Issues Lists** — operational issues that belong
   inside a function. These get worked at each department's own
   weekly meeting.

The leadership team should push department-level issues down to the
right Issues List rather than solving them at the L10. The L10 is for
strategic-altitude issues only.

## Criteria / Rules

**Hard rules (must pass):**

- Every IDS session follows the three-step structure (Identify /
  Discuss / Solve), in order, on every issue worked.
- Issues are worked in priority order, top-three-first — never strict
  top-to-bottom of the list.
- The Discuss step holds the one-turn rule (each person speaks once
  on the issue).
- Every Solve produces either a To-Do with a named owner, an explicit
  alignment statement, or an explicit "more research needed"
  assignment.
- The new To-Do List is read back during the Conclude segment so
  every owner has heard their commitment.
- The Issues List is maintained between meetings as a continuous
  queue, not generated at the start of the L10.

**Soft rules (context-dependent):**

- The issue type (problem / information / opportunity) is named at
  the start of Identify when the issue raiser remembers to do so.
- Some weeks the team works only one issue; other weeks they work
  ten. Both are acceptable.

## Common pitfalls

- **Solving the symptom.** The most expensive failure mode. Skipping
  Identify and jumping straight to action means the same issue
  returns the next week.
- **Starting at the top of the list.** Predicts that the most
  important issue never gets reached.
- **Saying it twice.** A leader who repeats their position after
  every other turn is politicking, not discussing — and the meeting
  leader needs to call it.
- **Solving without action.** A Solve that produces no To-Do, no
  alignment statement, and no research assignment is not actually a
  Solve. The issue will be back next week.
- **Tangent chains.** Failing to call drift in Discuss lets the team
  end up five topics away from the original issue with no one noticing.
- **Solving department-level issues at the L10.** Wastes leadership
  time and starves the department's own meeting of substance.
- **Skipping the type-naming step.** When the issue raiser does not
  state whether it is a problem, information, or an opportunity, the
  team chases the wrong resolution shape and burns time.

## When to apply

- Inside the sixty-minute IDS segment of every weekly L10.
- Inside the issues block of every Quarterly Planning Session.
- Inside the issues block of Day Two of every Annual Planning Session.
- Inside the issues block of every departmental weekly meeting once
  cascaded.
