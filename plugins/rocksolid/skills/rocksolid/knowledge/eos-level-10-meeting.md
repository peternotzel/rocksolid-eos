# The Level 10 Meeting

The Level 10™ Meeting (L10™) is the weekly heartbeat of an
Entrepreneurial Operating System™ (EOS™) implementation. It is a ninety-minute
meeting held on the same day at the same time every week, attended by the
leadership team and only the leadership team, and it is the forum where the
team confronts its numbers, its priorities, and its issues face to face.
Wickman's argument for why this meeting earns its place at the center of EOS
is straightforward: priorities and metrics that do not get reviewed weekly
quietly stop being priorities and metrics. The L10 is the forcing function
that keeps the Scorecard™, the Rocks™, the To-Do List, and the Issues List
all live and used rather than turning into stale documents in a shared
folder. Take the L10 away and the rest of the Traction Component slowly
deflates.

## What the L10 is for

The L10 exists to do four things in ninety minutes: surface every metric
that is off-track, surface every Rock that is off-track, capture customer
and employee signals from the past week, and resolve the most important
issues in front of the team using the Issues Solving Track (IDS™). The
ratio of those four jobs is deliberate — the first three together get
twenty-five minutes, while the fourth gets sixty. Most of the meeting is
issue resolution, because issue resolution is where leadership leverage
actually lives.

## The 7-item agenda with fixed time allocations

The agenda has exactly seven items in a fixed order with fixed time boxes:

| # | Agenda item                   | Time       |
|---|-------------------------------|------------|
| 1 | Segue                         | 5 minutes  |
| 2 | Scorecard review              | 5 minutes  |
| 3 | Rock review                   | 5 minutes  |
| 4 | Customer / employee headlines | 5 minutes  |
| 5 | To-Do List review             | 5 minutes  |
| 6 | IDS                           | 60 minutes |
| 7 | Conclude                      | 5 minutes  |
|   | **Total**                     | **90 min** |

The agenda is the same every week. The team does not redesign it, does not
shuffle the order, does not adjust the time boxes for "this week is
different." The point of locking it down is precisely to remove that
decision from the team's mental load.

### Item 1 — Segue (5 minutes)

A short transition ritual. Each person in the room shares one piece of good
news — business or personal — to mark the shift from working *in* the
business to working *on* it. The five minutes are not throwaway; they are a
deliberate context switch and a quick reminder that the people around the
table are humans rather than titles.

What does *not* happen here: discussion, questions, follow-ups, "tell me
more about that." A segue is a one-sentence share around the table.

### Item 2 — Scorecard review (5 minutes)

The team walks the five-to-fifteen weekly numbers on the Scorecard. For
each number, the owner says one of two words: on-track or off-track.
That is the entire content of this segment. Any number that missed its
goal drops immediately to the Issues List for handling later in IDS.

What does *not* happen here: discussion of why a number missed,
brainstorming about how to fix it, debate about whether the goal was
right. The instinct in every leadership team is to launch into
problem-solving the moment a bad number appears, and that instinct must
be suppressed. If the team gives in, the L10 blows past ninety minutes
and still solves nothing — because the truly important issue may have
been at the bottom of the list and never got reached.

### Item 3 — Rock review (5 minutes)

The team walks the Rock Sheet top to bottom: company Rocks first, then
each leadership member's individual Rocks. The owner of each Rock reports
one of two words: on-track or off-track. "On-track" means the owner is
genuinely confident of completing it by quarter-end, not that they hope
to. Off-track Rocks drop to the Issues List. An on-track Rock can also be
dropped to the Issues List if the owner or someone else wants to raise a
concern about it.

What does *not* happen here: discussion of progress, status updates,
explanations. Five minutes for the whole Rock Sheet means each Rock gets
a few seconds.

### Item 4 — Customer / employee headlines (5 minutes)

One-sentence updates on anything significant happening with customers or
employees this week — positive or negative. A happy major client mention,
a frustrated key employee, a product quality event, a recruiting win.
Good news gets a brief acknowledgment in the moment. Bad news gets
written onto the Issues List for IDS.

What does *not* happen here: full anecdotes, detailed customer stories,
emotional processing of an employee situation.

### Item 5 — To-Do List review (5 minutes)

Every action item from the previous week is read out and scored binary:
done or not done. Done items get struck off. Not-done items either move
to next week (if there's a clear reason it slipped one week) or get
escalated onto the Issues List (if they keep slipping). A To-Do is by
definition a seven-day action item — short-horizon, narrow, binary. The
target is around ninety percent completion week over week. An item
sitting on the To-Do list for more than two weeks is itself a signal
that something is wrong.

What does *not* happen here: re-litigating whether the To-Do should
have existed, discussing how to do the To-Do, or generating new To-Dos
(those come out of IDS in Item 6).

### Item 6 — IDS (60 minutes)

The core of the meeting. The team picks the top three issues from the
Issues List in priority order and works them one at a time using the
Identify / Discuss / Solve discipline. This segment gets sixty of the
ninety minutes because issue resolution is where leadership leverage
actually lives — see the IDS knowledge block for the mechanics.

What does *not* happen here: starting at the top of the Issues List and
working down. The team picks the top three by importance, which is often
not the same as the top three on the page.

### Item 7 — Conclude (5 minutes)

Three things happen in the closing five minutes. First, the meeting
leader reads back the new To-Do List that was generated during IDS, so
every owner has heard their own commitment stated out loud. Second, the
team decides which of today's decisions need to be communicated to the
rest of the organization, through which channel, and by whom. Third,
each attendee gives the meeting a 1-to-10 rating; the standing target
is an average above eight, and anything lower triggers a brief
conversation about what to fix next week.

What does *not* happen here: extending into a tenth or eleventh issue
because IDS ran out of time. The hard stop is enforced.

## Same day, same time, same agenda

The structural rule of the L10 is that it runs on the same day of the
week, at the same start time, with the same agenda, every week — without
exception. Wickman is uncompromising on this. Once a team starts moving
the L10 around to accommodate other things, the meeting begins decaying
out of existence within a couple of months. The consistency is what
removes the meeting from the team's mental load and turns it into a
fact of the week rather than a decision.

## Meeting roles — runner and scribe

Two roles are assigned at the start of the L10:

- **Meeting leader.** Runs the team through the agenda, watches the
  clock, enforces the time boxes, prompts each owner to report, and
  facilitates IDS during the sixty-minute segment.
- **Scribe / paper manager.** Keeps the Scorecard, the Rock Sheet, the
  To-Do List, and the Issues List visible and current in the room. Edits
  them live during the meeting — adding new To-Dos as IDS produces them,
  adding new issues to the list as they surface, updating Scorecard
  cells as numbers come in.

These are working roles, not honorary ones. The team functions noticeably
worse when either role is left unassigned.

## Pre-work

A real L10 depends on pre-work being done before the meeting starts:

- Rocks are set and the Rock Sheet is built.
- The Scorecard is filled in for the week ending before the meeting.
- The Issues List has been added to throughout the week (not generated
  in the meeting).
- Every attendee is fluent in the IDS discipline.

A team that walks into the L10 without these is going to spend the first
twenty minutes recreating documents and have no IDS time left.

## Attendance discipline

The L10 runs on its scheduled day at its scheduled time even when
members are missing. The team does not reschedule it. The team does not
skip it. Acceptable absences are roughly limited to vacation and
serious emergencies — Wickman's rule is deliberately framed in extreme
terms to make the point that the meeting is not optional and not
movable. The cost of moving the L10 once is that it becomes movable
forever.

## Start on time, end on time

Two punctuality rules are non-negotiable:

- **Start on time.** Arriving two or three minutes early is the only
  way to actually begin on the scheduled minute, because anything later
  eats into the meeting itself. Late starts cascade and the segment
  that gets sacrificed is always IDS — the most valuable one.
- **End on time.** When the L10 runs over its ninety-minute hard stop,
  it blows up the rest of every attendee's afternoon and trains the
  team to treat the meeting as elastic. Within a few weeks an elastic
  L10 is regularly running two hours and producing the same output it
  used to produce in ninety minutes.

The hard stop is a feature, not a constraint. It forces the team to
prioritize ruthlessly inside IDS rather than letting discussion sprawl.

## Other ground rules

- **Minutes are not taken.** The agenda document, the updated Rock
  Sheet, the updated Scorecard, the new To-Do List, and the updated
  Issues List collectively *are* the record. Separate minutes would
  duplicate work and never get read.
- **Devices off.** Laptops closed, phones face down. The leadership
  team gets ninety minutes of full presence per week with each other,
  and this is it.

## Criteria / Rules

**Hard rules (must pass for the L10 to count as installed):**

- All seven agenda items present in fixed order.
- Time allocations match the standard (5 / 5 / 5 / 5 / 5 / 60 / 5).
- The meeting runs the same day and time every week.
- A meeting leader and a scribe are assigned.
- The Rock Sheet, Scorecard, To-Do List, and Issues List are physically
  or digitally present and updated live during the meeting.
- IDS is given its full sixty minutes — not compressed when other items
  run over.
- The hard stop at ninety minutes is enforced.
- At least four consecutive weeks of L10s have been held with real
  content (not empty placeholders) before the Phase 1 cadence gate
  closes.

**Soft rules (context-dependent):**

- The 1-to-10 closing rating is recommended but not strictly required.
- Phase 1 teams may need two or three weeks to get the segment timings
  right; that is normal calibration.

## Common pitfalls

- **Discussing during the Scorecard or Rock review.** Pulls the team
  into solving on items that should drop to the Issues List for IDS,
  blows the time box, and crowds out IDS itself.
- **Starting at the top of the Issues List in IDS.** The most important
  issue is often near the bottom; starting at the top means the
  important issue never gets reached.
- **Letting the L10 run over its hard stop.** Trains the team to treat
  the meeting as elastic and predicts decay within weeks.
- **Moving the L10 to a different day "just this week."** Same as
  above — once is forever.
- **Skipping the L10 because key people are out.** The L10 runs without
  them. Skipping is not an option.
- **No assigned scribe.** The Rock Sheet, Scorecard, and Issues List
  silently fall out of date without one, and the next week's meeting
  has no working substrate.
- **Walking in without pre-work.** Recreating documents in the room
  destroys the meeting.

## When to apply

- Phase 1, immediately after Rocks and the Meeting Pulse calendar are
  set.
- Every week thereafter, on the locked day and time.
- During the cadence gate review — the skill should verify that at
  least four real L10s have been held before declaring Phase 1
  complete.
